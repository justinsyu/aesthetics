Scope caveat

MFN has meant different things in different proposals: the 2020 CMS Part B model would have tied certain high-cost Medicare Part B payment amounts to the lowest GDP-adjusted OECD price and was rescinded effective February 28, 2022,[4] while the 2025 executive order directed HHS to communicate MFN price targets and consider rulemaking, importation pathways, enforcement actions, and export review if progress was insufficient,[2] and 2026 White House research describes voluntary manufacturer agreements, new-launch pricing comparable to high-income countries, possible codification, and upward pressure on wealthy-nation prices.[1]

2025/2026 framework mechanism current frame

Targets

HHS was directed to communicate MFN price targets to manufacturers within 30 days of the May 12, 2025 executive order.[2]

Agreements

White House research reported voluntary MFN pricing agreements with 17 of the largest pharmaceutical manufacturers.[1]

Backstops

If progress is insufficient, the executive order lists possible rulemaking, importation pathways, enforcement actions, and export review.[2]

Stakeholder impacts run in opposite directions

Each box summarizes an evidence-backed directional impact from the cited policy estimates, administrative record, or survey data.
Patients
01

Potential savings are salient where coverage is absent or weak: White House research estimates GLP-1 users without coverage would save $3,000 per year and IVF couples more than $6,000.[1]

Need is visible in adherence data: 59% of adults were worried about affording prescription drugs in a March 2026 KFF poll, and 43% reported not taking medication as prescribed in the past year due to costs.[12]

Medicare beneficiaries
02

The 2020 CMS Part B MFN interim final rule estimated that aggregate beneficiary Part B cost-sharing would decrease and that Part B premiums would decrease; ASPE estimated beneficiary spending would fall by $33.2B over seven years.[5]

For context outside MFN, CMS reported that the first 10 negotiated Medicare Part D drugs accounted for $56.2B in gross Part D costs and $3.9B in beneficiary out-of-pocket costs in 2023.[9]

Federal and state payers
03

For the rescinded 2020 Part B model, OACT estimated $85.5B in Medicare Part B savings over 7 years net of the premium offset, and ASPE estimated an $87.8B net reduction across federal, state, and beneficiary spending.[5]

For the 2025/2026 framework, White House research estimates $529B in domestic savings over 10 years and $64.3B in federal/state Medicaid savings from existing drugs at MFN prices.[1]

Clinicians and hospitals
04

The 2020 Federal Register notice documented administrative costs for MFN participants from adjusting purchasing, changing pricing models, recordkeeping, tracking units excluded from ASPs, and spillover effects.[7]

CMS estimated annual clinician survey burden at $10.7M and hardship exemption submission burden at $1.5M, and stated that small rural hospitals would face significant impact.[7]

Manufacturers
05

White House research describes new U.S. launches at prices comparable to high-income countries and states that the framework aims for lower U.S. prices and upward pressure on wealthy-nation prices.[1]

CBO's preliminary H.R. 3 analysis estimated that revenue reductions of $0.5T-$1T would reduce new drugs by approximately 8-15 over 10 years, while foreign prices would rise and some launches abroad could be limited.[8]

Access risk
06

The 2020 Federal Register notice cautioned that if participants did not provide MFN drugs or prescribed alternatives, beneficiaries might need alternate providers, travel, receive lower-efficacy or higher-risk alternatives, postpone care, or forgo treatment.[6]

CMS stated that these access effects were uncertain and planned monitoring.[6]

Medicare context and negotiation baseline

In 2021-2022, 88.6% of adults ages 65+ took prescription medication, 3.6% did not get needed prescription medication due to cost, and 3.4% did not take medication as prescribed due to cost.[11]

For context, not MFN, CMS estimated that if negotiated prices for the first 10 Medicare Part D selected drugs had been in effect in 2023, Medicare would have saved $6B net and net spending would have been 22% lower; CMS projected $1.5B in savings for people with Medicare Part D coverage in 2026.[9]

In the March 2026 KFF poll, 27% of adults reported not filling a prescription and 19% reported cutting pills or skipping doses due to cost in the past year.[12]

Policy designs imply different operational tradeoffs

The design axis matters because the cited sources describe different statutory vehicles, populations, markets, and enforcement paths.

2020 CMS Part B model

The model would have paid no more for selected high-cost Medicare Part B drugs than the lowest GDP-adjusted OECD price and was not implemented; CMS rescinded the final rule effective February 28, 2022.[4]

The Federal Register notice estimated savings but also documented administrative burden, rural-provider impact, and uncertain access effects requiring monitoring.[5][6][7]

H.R. 3 reference-price ceiling

CBO described a ceiling not exceeding 120% of the average price in Australia, Canada, France, Germany, Japan, and the United Kingdom, and preliminarily estimated title I would reduce direct spending by $345B over 2023-2029.[8]

CBO also expected foreign prices to rise, some launches abroad to be limited, and lower manufacturer revenue to reduce new drug development by approximately 8-15 drugs over 10 years.[8]
