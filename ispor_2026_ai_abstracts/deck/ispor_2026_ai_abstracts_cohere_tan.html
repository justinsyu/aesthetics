Evidence synthesis

Literature-review abstracts described AI use in screening, extraction, assessment, and synthesis tasks

Screening and triage

Several records evaluated large language models or machine-learning classifiers for title and abstract screening, prioritization, citation triage, and reviewer workload reduction within systematic or targeted literature reviews

Structured extraction

Data-extraction abstracts focused on converting narrative evidence into structured fields, including study characteristics, comparator information, outcomes, quality-assessment inputs, and economic claims

Synthesis workflows

Some studies described AI-mediated support for indirect treatment comparisons, network meta-analysis feasibility checks, citation-chain assessment, and evidence-synthesis reporting

Across these records, human review and expert adjudication were commonly described as safeguards for model-generated screening, extraction, or synthesis outputs

Predictive analytics and real-world data

Predictive modeling abstracts used heterogeneous data sources and endpoints

Data sources and populations

  • Real-world evidence records described claims data, electronic health records, registries, patient journeys, hospital data, administrative data, or literature-derived datasets
  • Disease-specific examples included oncology, cardiovascular disease, metabolic disease, respiratory conditions, neurological disorders, rare diseases, and urinary or kidney disorders
  • Some abstracts addressed cross-disease methodological questions rather than a single clinical population

Endpoints and evaluation

  • Prediction tasks included adherence, treatment outcomes, disease progression, biopsy decisions, safety events, regulatory approval, and patient-risk stratification
  • Model-performance reporting commonly referenced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, F1 score, AUC, expert review, or comparison against human or conventional methods
  • Several abstracts treated model outputs as inputs requiring validation rather than as standalone evidence

HTA and economic evaluation

Decision-context abstracts emphasized evidence processing, model inputs, and assessment documentation

Observed use contexts

  • HTA and payer-oriented abstracts addressed joint clinical assessment, NICE technology appraisal content, value assessment, reimbursement-relevant evidence, and medical or economic claim evaluation
  • Economic-modeling records referenced cost-effectiveness, budget impact, health economic models, QALYs, Markov or survival modeling, and source traceability
  • Regulatory or access-adjacent abstracts generally framed AI as a method for evidence processing or prediction rather than as a replacement for formal assessment procedures

Validation and governance language

Abstracts frequently described human review, performance measurement, or quality controls

Human review

Records commonly described expert review, reviewer adjudication, human-in-the-loop workflows, or comparison of AI-assisted outputs with human-generated reference outputs

Performance metrics

Reported evaluation measures included conventional classification and prediction metrics, agreement statistics, usability assessments, processing-time comparisons, and error analyses

Responsible use themes

Some abstracts explicitly addressed bias, fairness, equity, transparency, explainability, hallucination risk, reproducibility, and alignment with HTA expectations

The extracted abstracts generally presented AI systems as tools requiring evaluation within defined data, task, and review contexts
