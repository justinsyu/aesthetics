    Abstract

    BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVE:

    A retrospective, noninterventional cohort study of the American Academy of Ophthalmology IRIS Registry, an electronic health record (EHR)-based comprehensive eye disease and condition registry, intended to assess whether the IRIS® Registry (Intelligent Research in Sight) could emulate the VIEW randomized clinical trials (VIEW RCTs) eligibility criteria, treatment protocol regimen, and primary endpoint.

    PATIENTS AND METHODS:

    Deidentified patients having an anti-VEGF injection of aflibercept or ranibizumab between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2018, from the IRIS Registry. Patients were treated in accordance with one of three treatment regimens from the VIEW RCT: monthly intravitreal aflibercept injection (IAI 2Q4), intravitreal aflibercept every 2 months after 3 initial monthly doses (IAI 2Q8), or monthly ranibizumab (RQ4) injection. The main outcome measures are the number and proportion of patients meeting VIEW RCT eligibility and treatment group criteria, demographic, and clinical differences between IRIS Registry treatment groups, mean change in best documented visual acuity at one year, and evaluation of the primary endpoint of the VIEW RCT: difference in the proportion of patients maintaining vision.

    RESULTS:

    Among the 90,900 patients who met VIEW RCT eligibility criteria, 4,457 (4.85%) met treatment group criteria. The percentage of patients maintaining vision at one year was over 90%. No statistically significant difference was observed when comparing the proportion of patients maintaining vision among the RQ4 treatment group to the IAI 2Q4 or IAI 2Q8 treatment group.

    CONCLUSIONS:

    A small percentage of real-world patients met VIEW RCT study eligibility criteria and treatment protocol regimen. Among patients meeting all available criteria, the primary endpoint interpretation yielded by an observational EHR-based dataset suggested comparable results to the VIEW RCT.

    The VEGF Trap-Eye: Investigation of Efficacy and Safety in Wet AMD (VIEW 1 and VIEW 2) trials were pivotal parallel trials (the VIEW RCTs) that led to approval of aflibercept to treat neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), a leading cause of vision loss and blindness.1 Using electronic health record (EHR)- derived data from the IRIS® Registry (Intelligent Research in Sight), containing over 2.1 million nAMD patients. We evaluated whether EHR data could produce similar results as the VIEW RCTs by identifying patients with similar eligibility criteria and treatment regimens.

    We assessed whether the IRIS Registry could emulate the VIEW RCT eligibility criteria, treatment protocol regimen,2–4 and primary endpoint after accounting for missing endpoint data and population differences. In addition, we evaluated baseline differences between the IRIS Registry treatment groups and mean change in VA at one year from starting treatment.

    METHODS

    The VIEW RCTs were phase-3 double-masked, multicenter, parallel-group, active controlled, randomized trials that enrolled 2,419 patients with active, treatment-naive nAMD (Table 1).5 Both aflibercept treatment groups were noninferior to monthly ranibizumab and over 94% of patients maintained vision after one year (losing less than 3 lines of vision one year from their baseline visual acuity (VA) measurement).

    STUDY POPULATION

    We used deidentified EHR data from the American Academy of Ophthalmology IRIS Registry, which is an EHR-based comprehensive eye disease and condition registry in the United States that contains over 422 million patient visits from 71 million patients over 8 years (data on file, Verana Health) (Figure 1). The Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved this study.

    ENDPOINTS

    VA was defined as the best documented (BDVA) Snellen reading after conversion to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution units (logMAR).6,7 VA at one year was defined as the VA measure one year (365 days) from the patient’s baseline VA date, with a buffer of ±14 days. If multiple VA observations were made for one patient, their first VA observation was included. VA conversion methods, including conversion of logMAR units to letters, followed previous literature.8,9 As defined in the VIEW RCT, maintaining vision was defined as having vision loss less than 15 letters (or less than 3 lines of vision) one year from their baseline BDVA measurement.

    The three methodologies applied to the primary endpoint, described in detail below, included: (1) complete case analysis, thereby excluding individuals with missing 1-year VA data; (2) multiple imputation unadjusted by transportability weight; and (3) multiple imputation adjusted by transportability weight.10,11

    ELIGIBLE STUDY POPULATION FROM VIEW RCT ELIGIBILITY CRITERIA

    A board-certified ophthalmologist who has completed an AUPO FCC compliant retina fellowship conducted a review of the VIEW RCT eligibility and developed the treatment criteria a priori to assess derivation, availability, and fit for use of variables from the IRIS Registry. Based on this review, VIEW RCT inclusion and exclusion requirements were applied (Table 2).5 The index date was defined as the first injection date. From this initial eligible cohort, we eliminated patient eyes to match the VIEW RCT (Table 2).     SYNTHETIC VIEW RCT STUDY POPULATION

    Previous research has compared observational outcomes with results from RCTs after applying a transportability weighting method using group means as opposed to individual subjects.9 However, this method does not account for correlation between clinically relevant variables. For this reason, we created a synthetic VIEW RCT population by randomly sampling from a normal distribution of the baseline characteristics given the VIEW RCTs summary statistics (mean and standard deviation) for each treatment group and study for the following variables: age and baseline visual acuity in letters.5 Due to the clinical expectation that baseline VA would be correlated with age, we computed the correlation coefficient between VA and age among all nAMD patients in the IRIS Registry between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2018, at the date of their first nAMD diagnosis among patients with a VA reading on or 30 days prior to the diagnosis date. The calculated correlation coefficient (r = 0.20) was then used to reconstruct the synthetic population using the Cholesky decomposition method.12 Briefly, the Cholesky decomposition method transforms uncorrelated random Gaussian variables into simulated correlated normal variables.13

    TRANSPORTABILITY WEIGHT ADJUSTMENT

    To transport the effect from what we observed in the IRIS Registry population to a population similar to the VIEW RCT population, our unadjusted difference in proportion maintaining vision effect estimates were weighted using the inverse-odds-of-sampling approach outlined by Westreich et al.11 The transportability weight adjustment method has been considered to be analogous to propensity score weighting.     MULTIPLE IMPUTATION METHOD

    To impute missing logMAR VA measurements 1-year post baseline, we performed multiple imputation with a multivariate pan algorithm Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) technique using observed VA data and a statistical model.15,16 The statistical model consisted of a mixed-effects model with age (as a continuous variable), days from index date, an interaction term of the two variables, and unique patient identifier as the random intercept. The MCMC procedure first used a burn-in phase to stabilize estimation parameters and an imputation phase to draw the replacement for missing VA values into the imputed datasets.16 As recommended by Zhao and Grund, we performed 50,000 burn-in iterations with 5,000 iterations separating each of the 25 imputations.16 Subsequent calculations deriving pooled mean proportion differences and the standard error calculations followed Rubin’s rules to account for within and between imputation variances.17

    STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

    Characteristic differences between the VIEW RCTs versus IRIS Registry treatment groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests for continuous variables, and chi-squared or Fisher’s exact for categorical variables. Differences were considered statistically significant if P < 0.05 (two-sided). Analyses were performed with PySpark software (Apache Software Foundation) version 2.4.0 and R software version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

    RESULTS

    ELIGIBLE STUDY POPULATION FROM VIEW RCT INCLUSION/EXCLUSION CRITERIA

    From the IRIS Registry, we were able to meet 12 out of the 18 (66.7%) VIEW RCT eligibility criteria. The exclusion criteria not applied in our study are shown in Table 2.

    There was a total of 606,971 patients who had an anti-VEGF injection during our study period. After applying attainable eligibility criteria from the VIEW RCT, we excluded a total of 516,071 patients (85.0%), which left 90,900 patients in our eligible study population (Table B, available in the online version of this article). The largest attrition in patients occurred from requiring the presence of a nAMD diagnosis code within 6 months preceding the first injection date, which reduced the number of eligible patients from 606,971 to 221,006 patients, or a 63.6% drop.

    APPLICATION OF VIEW RCT TREATMENT GROUP CREATION

    Among the 90,900 patients in the eligible study population, 4,457 (4.9%) patients met the specified treatment group criteria. Of these 4457 patients, 2,520 (56.5%) met the IAI 2Q4 treatment group criteria, 154 (3.5%) IAI 2Q8 group, and 1,783 (40.0%) RQ4 group.

    DISCUSSION

    Among patients that met the study eligibility criteria, we found the majority of patients did not fit into one of the three VIEW RCT treatment regimens. Compared to the VIEW RCT patient population, IRIS Registry patients meeting the treatment regimens were slightly older, more likely to be female, white, and have better baseline VA. Across all treatment groups and analyses, we found over 90% of patients-maintained vision after one year. Although we were unable to fully emulate the entire VIEW RCT exactly, the primary endpoint interpretation was identical: IAI 2Q4 and IAI 2Q8 treatment groups had similar efficacy as the RQ4 treatment group.

    In the current study, the primary barriers to fully replicating the VIEW RCTs were the inability to apply eligibility criteria based on imaging data, difficulty identifying patients with a baseline VA between 20/40 and 20/320, limited numbers of patients who had similar treatment protocol regimens to the VIEW RCTs, imbalanced baseline characteristics compared to the VIEW RCTs treatment groups, and differing proportions of patients with one year VA measurements by treatment group. The acquisition of imaging data could be used to augment clinical trial replications for future studies; however, imaging data was not available for this study and may have been a source of bias during cohort building.

    The large percentage of patients excluded due to baseline VA outside the bounds of the VIEW RCTs reflects the real-world nature of baseline VA for those starting anti-VEGF treatment. Additionally, we found that a high percentage of patients did not follow one of the three prescribed VIEW RCT treatment regimens. This observation has been made in previous research with the IRIS Registry and led to a relative imbalance of treatment group numbers in this study with the 2Q8 being smaller than the other two groups.18 We attempted to address the last two barriers by applying a transportability weight adjustment method and evaluating different missing data assumptions for VA. The current study adds to recent research where many study criteria were available in the IRIS Registry, and several statistically significant treatment group differences were observed in the IRIS Registry treatment groups but not in the clinical trial treatment groups.4

    All analyses showed that a slightly higher proportion of patients maintained vision in the IAI treatment groups compared to the RQ4 treatment group, although this difference in proportions was not statistically significant for any of our analyses. Both of these results were also found in the VIEW RCTs. These results confirm previous research with the IRIS Registry that suggested monotherapy with bevacizumab, ranibizumab, or aflibercept for one year improved visual acuity similarly.19

    In the VIEW RCT, the proportion of patients maintaining vision, the primary endpoint, varied between 94% to 96%, whereas the current study varied between 91% to 94%.5 Mean VA change, a secondary endpoint of the VIEW RCT, was approximately five letters (or one line of vision) lower in this study (complete case analysis) compared to the VIEW RCT, which may be the result of differences in baseline VA. A larger percentage of patients in the IRIS Registry population had a vision of ≥ 20/40 (range: 20.0% to 26.6%) compared to those in the VIEW RCTs (range: 2.6% to 6.3%) (Table 3). As a consequence of having better baseline VA, the IRIS Registry patient population may have a greater opportunity to lose VA but less opportunity to gain VA due to a ceiling effect. The ability to emulate the primary endpoint of the VIEW RCT more closely than the secondary endpoint highlights the importance of clearly specifying the objectives of RCT emulation efforts, including those assessed in this study.20

    Differences observed between RCT results relative to those yielded by observational studies, including those leveraging EHR-based datasets, may be driven by estimating a true and accurate effect that is applicable to a distinct target population from that of the RCT given variances in emulation steps (eg, applicability of patient eligibility requirements) as opposed to confounding bias. While RCT emulation efforts build confidence in the insights derived from observational studies, expansion of future studies to include more generalizable patients would be valuable.21

    In the VIEW RCT, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was measured for each patient by certified examiners on consistent vision charts (ETDRS VA chart with refraction). In our study, VA was obtained for patients within the IRIS Registry by extracting Snellen VA chart measurements sourced from a practice’s EHR. While visual acuity measurements from EHRs are less standardized compared to RCT BCVA measurements, they are an accurate reflection of real-world clinical practice, and a medication’s effectiveness in this context is important to understand. Of note, in one study, visual acuity measurements were better when obtained from ETDRS charts compared to Snellen charts.22 This may suggest VA values from Snellen charts without an updated refraction would more likely be an underestimate of a patient’s true visual potential; however, this bias would be present in each treatment group in the current study.

    The transportability weight adjustment marginally decreased the percentage of patients maintaining vision by less than 1 percentage point across all treatment groups and comparisons. Therefore, according to our specified model, weighting the IRIS Registry patient population to be similar to the VIEW RCT treatment protocol did not substantially impact the proportion of patients maintaining vision. Given the observational and retrospective nature of the IRIS Registry, we cannot rule out unobserved variables being associated with the treatment group and impacting our treatment effect estimates, especially if such variables were related to VA progression and differed between treatment groups.21 Furthermore, the use of the transportability weight adjustment is based on both an exchangeability and positivity assumption.7,13 For the latter, the positivity assumption in our study means that the probability of being included in the IRIS Registry population must be greater than zero for participants in all strata of our transportability weight adjustment model in the VIEW RCT.11

    LIMITATIONS

    Similar to previous studies, we were unable to fully capture all VIEW RCT inclusion and exclusion criteria largely because there were no available imaging data or applicable ICD/CPT codes.4,23 Among the six exclusion criteria that the study could not address, five criteria required access to patient images (ie, OCT and FA images). We do not expect any systematic imbalance between treatment groups if imaging criteria could be applied, nor do we expect a high proportion of patients would have been excluded. However, access to imaging data would also allow for assessment of the secondary anatomical endpoints from the VIEW RCT. For the study eligibility that we could apply, we believe the construct validity was strong, but standards will need to be agreed upon in each domain when translating RCT eligibility criteria to real-world cohorts. We also found that the real-world patients in the IRIS Registry do not typically follow a strict protocol of q4 or q8 weeks, but rather are treated on a treat-and-extend regimen. Additionally, the real-world monthly treatment group may represent nonresponders, rather than a choice of the treating physician to maintain them on continuous therapy. Thus, the outcomes may have been skewed due to this potential bias. Lastly, the results may have also been impacted due to the fact that many patients did not have a visual acuity measurement at the one-year mark; however, as stated above, we addressed this using a multiple imputation with a multivariate pan algorithm MCMC technique using observed VA data and a statistical model imputation method.

    Another limitation of the current study occurs from the information available from the VIEW RCTs. When creating the synthetic VIEW RCT study population, only summary statistics (ie, mean, and standard deviation) of several baseline characteristics were provided. For this reason, we assumed a normal distribution for continuous variables (eg, age). Having deidentified, individual-level patient data would fully capture the VIEW RCT patient population baseline characteristics and more accurately reflect correlations between baseline variables.

    CONCLUSIONS

    We were able to capture most, but not all, study eligibility from the VIEW RCTs with the IRIS Registry. While we found a substantial number of patients who did not fall into one of the three VIEW RCT treatment group regimens, our study still yielded a study population size capable of emulating the primary endpoint. We found no statistically significant difference in the proportion of patients maintaining vision between treatment groups.
