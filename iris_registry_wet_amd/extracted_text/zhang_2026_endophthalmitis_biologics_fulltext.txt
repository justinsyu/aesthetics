Abstract
Introduction

Intravitreal (IVT) injections are now among the most frequently performed intraocular procedures globally. Evidence regarding post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis is primarily derived from a limited number of randomized controlled trials and retrospective case series. This study assesses visual acuity outcomes after IVT biologic therapy utilizing data from a large-scale ophthalmic registry, with the objective of elucidating the real-world effects of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis on patient visual function.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Intelligent Research in Sight® (IRIS®) Registry, focusing on subjects who received commercially available IVT anti-VEGF and anti-complement biologic drugs.

Results

As of 31 December 2024, 1,998,399 individuals received at least one IVT injection in one or both eyes, and 13,074 subjects (affecting 13,317 eyes) were diagnosed with infectious endophthalmitis. Incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per IVT injection was 0.052%. The cumulative rate of infectious endophthalmitis per subject rose from 0.31% after 10 IVT injections to 0.58% after 60 injections. The proportion of subjects who were legally blind (defined as ≤ 35 letters or ≤ 20/200) increased from 19.4% before the infectious endophthalmitis event to 46.1% afterward. In total, 94 eyes affected by infectious endophthalmitis underwent evisceration or enucleation. Subjects with retinal vein occlusion and myopic choroidal neovascularization had the highest incidence rates of infectious endophthalmitis compared with those with neovascular (wet) age-related macular degeneration and diabetic macular edema/diabetic retinopathy.

Conclusions

In the real world, the cumulative incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per subject is positively correlated with the total number of IVT injections administered. While the incidence of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis was comparable to literature values reported after cataract surgery, visual acuity outcomes were worse in the post-IVT injection group. Implementing strategies aimed at reducing IVT injection frequency may have the potential to lower the incidence of infectious endophthalmitis and the consequent risk of significant vision loss.

Introduction

Intravitreal (IVT) injections have become one of the most common intraocular procedures performed, particularly with the increasing use of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents for retinal diseases [1]. Owing to the increased utilization [2], IVT injections are now associated with the highest absolute incidence rate of infectious endophthalmitis compared with other intraocular procedures such as cataract surgery [3]. Infectious endophthalmitis is a severe inflammatory condition of the intraocular tissues, predominantly caused by bacterial and fungal pathogens [4]. It represents a sight-threatening emergency that can rapidly progress, leading to substantial vision impairment or permanent blindness if not diagnosed and treated effectively [5]. Existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses on post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis often include only a small number of randomized controlled trials alongside retrospective case series [4, 6]. There have been a few landmark studies on infectious endophthalmitis, such as the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study (EVS) [7] and the European Society of Cataract & Refractive Surgeons (ESCRS) multicenter study [8], but these studies were focused on infectious endophthalmitis following cataract surgery and/or secondary intraocular lens, generally one-time events. A subject with retinal disease being treated with biologic drugs is expected to have frequent IVT injections over their lifetime, exposing them to higher risks of infectious endophthalmitis and vision loss. Large patient registries offer several advantages over traditional retrospective studies, despite both relying on retrospective data. Registries generally encompass substantially larger and more diverse subject populations sourced from real-world clinical environments, thereby enhancing the generalizability of results [9]. Data acquisition in registries is typically standardized and longitudinal, facilitating comprehensive analyses of temporal trends and clinical outcomes [10]. The objective of the current study was to gain a better understanding of the impact of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis on vision in real-world clinical settings using a large ophthalmic patient registry.

Methods

The American Academy of Ophthalmology Intelligent Research in Sight (IRIS®) Registry served as the data source for this retrospective study. The IRIS® Registry aggregates clinical data from the electronic health records (EHRs) of subjects assessed in participating US ophthalmology practices [11]. The analysis was conducted using de-identified, publicly accessible data collected as part of routine clinical practice from the IRIS® Registry. Since our study is retrospective and observational using de-identified data from the IRIS Registry and does not qualify as clinical trial according to FDA regulation 42 CFR Part 11, NIH, or ICMJE definitions, registering this study on ClinicalTrials.gov was not required.

Identification of IVT biologic drug administrations was performed using Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes (i.e., J codes). Infectious endophthalmitis was identified using ICD-9 codes of 360.00, 360.01, 360.03, and 360.19, as well as ICD-10 codes H44.0, H44.001, H44.002, H44.003, and H44.19. Enucleation and evisceration events were identified using the following ICD-9 codes: 65101, 65103, and 65105, and ICD-10 code Z90.01. Comparisons of incidence rates of infectious endophthalmitis between drugs were tested using Chi-squared tests for pairwise comparisons with Holm-adjusted p-values. An analysis was conducted to summarize infectious endophthalmitis incidence rates stratified by the four major indications receiving anti-VEGFs in the IRIS Registry: neovascular (wet) age related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular edema/diabetic retinopathy (DME/DR), retinal vein occlusion (RVO), and myopic choroidal neovascularization (CNV). To ensure clarity, our stratified analysis considered only eyes with a single disease category at the time of the first IVT injection, and we excluded eyes that had ICD codes, for example, of both nAMD and DME. The ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes used to identify the diseases are located in Table S1 in the Supplementary Material. Comparisons of incidence rates of infectious endophthalmitis between disease categories were tested using pairwise analyses with Holm-adjusted p-values.

In accordance with FDA exemption 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2)(i), the use of de-identified clinical data does not require patient consent, and institutional review board (IRB) or ethics committee approval is not required. The research adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. The manuscript was written following the principles and guidelines set by the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) in its research and publication practices. The research also complied with our institutional ethical standards of research reporting to uphold standards related to transparency, integrity, authorship, disclosure of conflicts of interest, and ethical conduct in publishing.

Results

From 8 August 2013 to 31 December 2024, the cohort identified from the IRIS® Registry comprised 15,236,452 distinct individuals. The pharmacologic agents evaluated in this analysis encompassed all anti-VEGF and anti-complement therapeutics that maintained commercial availability within the USA as of 31 December 2024. Included biologic therapies spanned bevacizumab, which is utilized off-label for IVT administration; however, the IRIS® Registry lacked granularity to distinguish between reference product Avastin (Genentech, Inc., South San Francisco, CA) and biosimilar formulations. Other biologics assessed comprised: ranibizumab (Lucentis®, Genentech, Inc., South San Francisco, CA), ranibizumab (Susvimo™, Genentech, Inc., South San Francisco, CA, USA), ranibizumab-nuna (Byooviz®, Biogen, Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA), ranibizumab-eqrn (Cimerli®, Sandoz, Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA), aflibercept 2 mg (Eylea®, Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc., Tarrytown, NY, USA), aflibercept 8 mg (Eylea® HD, Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc., Tarrytown, NY, USA), Brolucizumab (Beovu®, Novartis Pharmaceuticals, East Hanover, NJ, USA), faricimab (Vabysmo®, Genentech, Inc., South San Francisco, CA, USA), pegcetacoplan (Syfovre®, Apellis Pharmaceuticals, Waltham, MA, USA), and avacincaptad pegol (Izervay™, Astellas Pharma US, Inc., Northbrook, IL, USA).

In this data set, a total of 1,998,399 subjects received at least one IVT injection in one or both eyes, and 1,537,461 subjects (2,195,271 eyes) with detailed information on which a specific eye received an IVT injection. Overall, 13,074 subjects (13,317 eyes) had a diagnosis of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis (Table 1). In total, 25,845,527 IVT injections were recorded: for eyes without infectious endophthalmitis, all injections were counted; for eyes with infectious endophthalmitis, only injections administered before the diagnosis were included. Out of 13,074 subjects, infectious endophthalmitis was diagnosed in the right eye of 6451 subjects, in the left eye of 6380 subjects, and in both eyes of 243 subjects. All combined, the median follow-up from the time of the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) before the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis to the final BCVA, median (IQR): 912 (360–1769) days. Combining the data from all biologic drugs, the incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per IVT injection was a mean of 0.052% (0.051, 0.052, 95% CI). The drugs with the highest incidence of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis were brolucizumab (0.101%), aflibercept HD, 8 mg (0.073%), faricimab (0.061%), and aflibercept 2 mg (0.056%). The drugs with the highest number of IVT injections in this dataset were aflibercept 2 mg (10,689,347 injections), bevacizumab (8,394,754), and ranibizumab (4,475,299), which is consistent with the physician prescribing trends over the past decade [12]. Brolucizumab post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis rates were significantly higher than the rates of all three of these high utilization drugs (p < 0.001). Aflibercept HD, 8 mg had significantly higher infectious endophthalmitis rates than bevacizumab and ranibizumab (p < 0.001), and modestly higher than aflibercept 2 mg (p = 0.043). Faricimab had infectious endophthalmitis rates significantly higher than both bevacizumab and ranibizumab with p < 0.001. Similarly, aflibercept 2 mg had infectious endophthalmitis rates significantly higher than both bevacizumab and ranibizumab (p < 0.001).

The cumulative rate of infectious endophthalmitis per subject increased from 0.31% after ten IVT injections to 0.58% after 60 injections (Fig. 1). The median (IQR) time from the last injection of the causative biologic drug to the infectious endophthalmitis onset was 6 (3–41) days, with no clinically significant differences in onset time across the different drugs. The number of IVT injections until the infectious endophthalmitis event was 10 (4–23) days. Among 13,317 eyes, 13,256 had BCVA measurements taken before the last biologic drug injection prior to infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis, 13,114 had BCVA measurement after the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis, and 13,058 had BCVA measurements both before and after the diagnosis. The most recent mean BCVA before the last biologic drug injection before the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis was 58 ± 24 (range, 0–95) letters (~20/70 Snellen equivalent), and the worst mean BCVA after the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis was 8 ± 19 (range, 0–85) letters (~20/800 Snellen equivalent) (Table 2). The final mean BCVA recorded after the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis was 39 ± 31 (range, 0–95) letters (~20/160 Snellen equivalent), with a mean follow up of 1150 ± 943 (range, 3–4362) days or approximately 3.2 years. To further understand the degree of BCVA change that occurred after the diagnosis of infectious endophthalmitis, and using the BCVA before the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis as baseline, with all drugs combined, 3330/13,114 (25.4%) had a final visual acuity ≥ 20/40 (Table 3). The number of eyes that were legally blind at the final BCVA was 6042 of 13,114 (46.1%) eyes. Since 2575/13,256 (19.4%) eyes were already legally blind at baseline before the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis, and 6042 eyes were legally blind at the final BCVA assessment, this represents a 142% increase in eyes that were legally blind after the infectious endophthalmitis diagnosis. Very severe visual impairment can be defined as having a final mean BCVA of 0 letters, which translates from a practical viewpoint to being able to count fingers (CF) or see hand motion (HM) only [13], or worse if they were ‘light perception’ (LP) or ‘no light perception’ (NLP) only (which was not recorded in the IRIS® Registry). At baseline, 1127 out of 13,256 eyes (8.5%) had a visual acuity of 0 letters prior to the diagnosis of infectious endophthalmitis, while at the final BCVA assessment, 4053 out of 13,114 eyes (30.9%) were reading 0 letters. This indicates a 260% increase, with 2926 additional eyes unable to read any letters following the diagnosis. In total, 94 eyes affected by infectious endophthalmitis underwent evisceration or enucleation.

Discussion

The overall incidence of infectious endophthalmitis after IVT injection is low, generally reported in the range of 0.016–0.131% per injection in case reports and meta-analyses [14,15,16,17,18,19,20]. Higher rates have been associated with multi-use vials versus prefilled syringes [18]. In our study, the incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per IVT injection was 0.052%, which is consistent with other reports with smaller sample sizes. The incidence of infectious endophthalmitis following cataract surgery in larger studies is estimated to range in total from 0.04% [21] to 0.136% [8], which is consistent with the range observed with IVT injections.

Frequent IVT anti-VEGF injections remain the standard of care for nAMD; however, they present a significant treatment burden, and undertreatment can lead to progressive vision loss [22]. More intensive or proactive treatment strategies can help sustain initial visual acuity improvements by encouraging subjects and providers to persist with anti-VEGF therapy beyond the first year [23]. Continued frequent injections, however, may increase the cumulative risk of developing sight-threatening infectious endophthalmitis. Few studies have longitudinal data to examine the cumulative rates of infectious endophthalmitis per subject [21]. In our study, the cumulative incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per subject was found to be positively correlated with the total number of IVT injections administered. In fact, the number of IVT injections in a subject undergoing intensive therapy over their lifetime (for example, 30–60 injections over 5–6 years) was an order of magnitude higher compared with receiving just a single injection. This is an important consideration when encouraging subjects to continue with IVT therapy when the risk/benefit may change over time when the risk of infectious endophthalmitis increases with each injection administered.

It is challenging to compare visual acuity outcomes across different studies following IVT injections because each study may report changes in vision, both gains and losses, using different methods. Some meta-analyses studies reviewing published reports with large number of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis subjects have limited visual acuity information [15, 16]. Claims-based studies primarily examine incidence rates of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis and do not have access to visual acuity information [24]. We reviewed publications reporting visual acuity data with a minimum of 6 months of follow-up, consolidating cases that included final visual acuity outcomes [20, 25,26,27,28]. Of 335 eyes reported in these publications, 86 (26%) had ≤ 5/200 (i.e., 5 ETDRS letters), which also included CF, HM, LP, and NLP eyes. Of 158 eyes, 41 (26%) had a final visual acuity ≥ 20/40. Our study showed with all eyes with post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis combined, 4159 /13,114 (31.7%) had final visual acuities of ≤ 5/200, and 3330/13,114 (25.4%) had a final visual acuity of ≥ 20/40. Our visual outcomes are comparable to what has been reported in literature with post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis.

In the assessment of visual acuity outcomes for infectious endophthalmitis cases associated with cataract extraction and/or secondary intraocular lens implantation from literature, where studies included a mean follow-up period of ≥ 6 months, the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study provides the most extensive dataset [12]. Within this cohort (n = 394 eyes), 45 eyes (11%) attained a final visual acuity ≤ 5/200, while 209 eyes (53%) achieved a visual acuity ≥ 20/40 [29]. A separate cohort study examining infectious endophthalmitis following cataract surgery (n = 73 eyes) reported a final visual acuity of ≤ 5/200 in 13 eyes (18%) and ≥ 20/40 in 35 eyes (48%) [30]. A large study using a patient registry with 3629 cases of post-cataract infectious endophthalmitis also demonstrated favorable vision results, with 44% achieving 20/40 or better visual acuity at 3 months [10]. Collectively, these data suggest that visual acuity outcomes after cataract surgery associated with infectious endophthalmitis are more favorable compared with those observed with post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis. This observation may appear paradoxical, as IVT injections involve significantly less surgical manipulation and shorter procedure times than cataract surgery. In general, surgical practice, infection rates are known to rise progressively with increasing operative duration, typically in 15-min intervals [31]. Similarly, in ophthalmic surgery, phacoemulsification is associated with a lower risk of infectious endophthalmitis compared with extracapsular cataract extraction, likely owing to its smaller incision size and decreased surgical duration times [32]. There may be other factors involved such as contamination of the injection site by saliva droplets from the surgeon as (s)he is speaking with the awake subject or staff. Notably, streptococcal species (spp.) constitute approximately 41% of the human salivary microbiota. While Staphylococcus spp. are most frequently isolated in post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis, Streptococcus spp. infections are observed at three times the rate with IVT injections compared with other intraocular surgeries and are known to be associated with a worse visual prognosis [15, 33, 34]. Finally, adherence to strict sterility protocols is greater during cataract surgeries conducted in an operating room environment. According to a survey involving retinal specialists’ in-office IVT injection practices [35], 39% did not use a surgical mask or gloves, and 97% omitted sterile surgical draping; both measures are standard in the operating room setting. Evidence showing a consistently low rate of infectious endophthalmitis following IVT injections performed in the operating room [36] implies that relaxing sterility standards in office-based procedures may increase the risk of infection.

A significant concern for subjects with infectious endophthalmitis is not only the potential for vision loss but also the risk of losing the eye. In our study of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis, the enucleation rate was 0.71% (94/13,317 cases). Similarly, in the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study [7], which evaluated cases of infectious endophthalmitis following cataract surgery or secondary intraocular lens (IOL) implantation, the enucleation rate was 0.72% (3/420 cases). Although neither our study or the Endophthalmitis Vitrectomy Study provided culture results corresponding to eye removals, data from a separate study of 101 infectious endophthalmitis cases (including 48 post-cataract surgery and 53 post-IVT injection) indicated that streptococcal spp. infections were associated with an increased risk of enucleation [33]. In addition, infections with more virulent organisms are associated with higher rates of enucleation in bleb-associated infectious endophthalmitis, with reported rates as high as 13% in a cohort of 71 eyes [39]. In a study with 45 cases of infectious endophthalmitis from a variety of causes (e.g., postoperative, endogenous, and post-traumatic), 15 (33.3%) cases ended in eye removal, and all were culture positive for streptococcal spp. [37].

In addition to microbiological factors, poorer visual prognosis with post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis is also influenced by the underlying ocular condition typically present in subjects receiving anti-VEGF therapy. These subjects frequently have preexisting retinal diseases, such as DME or nAMD, which are associated with reduced baseline visual acuity and compromised retinal architecture. As a result, there may be diminished visual reserve to withstand further damage from infection and inflammation [38]. Conversely, subjects undergoing cataract surgery often have healthier ocular baselines and may lack severe retinal pathology, contributing to more favorable visual outcomes when infectious endophthalmitis occurs postoperatively. In contrast, advanced age, typical of the subject that has nAMD and receiving anti-VEGF therapy, is an independent risk factor for poor visual prognosis in infectious endophthalmitis [39]. Collectively, healthier baseline ocular status and the absence of significant retinal disease may account for the superior visual outcomes observed in post-cataract surgery infectious endophthalmitis compared with post-IVT injection cases. Lastly, post-IVT injection follow-up usually consists of subjects returning for subsequent monthly or bimonthly retinal examinations and additional injections, with earlier visits advised only if symptoms develop. By comparison, cataract surgery is followed by a more structured schedule that facilitates earlier detection of infection or postoperative complications [40].

Other studies investigating the propensity of different anti-VEGFs to cause post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis have shown mixed results. Using health claims data, one study found aflibercept 2 mg [20] had an incidence of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis of 0.025% for fflibercept 2 mg injection and 0.014% for ranibizumab, and the same conclusions were found with other studies using health claims data [29, 41]. However, other studies have not shown a statistically significant difference in post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis rates between aflibercept 2 mg and ranibizumab [30,31,32]. Our study showed a highly significant difference in post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis rates of aflibercept 2 mg over ranibizumab, and this may be due to the very large sample size available through the IRIS® Registry to be able to show a statistical difference. However, the reason for the higher incidence of infectious endophthalmitis with aflibercept 2 mg versus ranibizumab is not readily apparent. Prefilled syringes for ranibizumab and aflibercept 2 mg were introduced in 2016 and 2019, respectively. The use of prefilled syringes has been associated with a reduced incidence of infectious endophthalmitis, likely owing to fewer preparation steps required for injection, thereby minimizing opportunities for contamination [22, 38]. During the study period spanning 2013–2024, aflibercept 2 mg may have contributed a comparatively shorter duration of data to the IRIS® Registry specifically regarding injections administered via prefilled syringes. Aflibercept 2 mg contains an Fc domain that can act as a proinflammatory factor, which may contribute to significantly higher rates of severe intraocular inflammation observed with aflibercept 2 mg versus ranibizumab [29]. Some severe cases of intraocular inflammation may have been sterile in nature yet presented with clinical features mimicking infectious endophthalmitis owing to their severe presentations. This phenomenon may also contribute to the observed higher rates of infectious endophthalmitis with brolucizumab, which is associated with an increased risk of severe sterile inflammation relative to other commercially available anti-VEGF agents [35, 42].

We hypothesized that patients with diabetes may be at increased risk for postoperative infections such as infectious endophthalmitis, as previously reported following cataract surgery [43]. However, contrary to expectations, the DME/DR cohort exhibited the lowest rate of infectious endophthalmitis. While current literature does not recognize RVO as an independent risk factor for infectious endophthalmitis, this group showed the highest incidence relative to the other categories in our analysis. Additional investigations are needed to clarify the relationships between underlying disease states, anti-VEGF agents, and infectious endophthalmitis risk.

Although it has been debatable for decades, facemasks have been used to reduce surgical site contamination from oral flora with surgical procedures in other therapeutics areas [44] and more recently with IVT injections [45, 46]. Although the use of face masks for IVT injections remains controversial [28], current preferred practices patterns with IVT injections universally recommend the use of povidone iodine prior to the injection to reduce the risk of infectious endophthalmitis [47]. As previously stated, prefilled syringes may also reduce the manipulation of the drug from vials and reduce post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis rates [27]. Consistent with this may be reports of an increase in post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis rates using bevacizumab, used off-label for IVT use, which is produced through compounding pharmacies. Multiple large retrospective databases and registry analyses have conducted direct comparisons of infectious endophthalmitis risk across individual pharmacologic agents. In one such study, the real-world incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per 10,000 injections was reported as 3.64 for bevacizumab, 1.39 for ranibizumab, and 0.76 for aflibercept; bevacizumab demonstrated a statistically higher incidence relative to the other agents [48]. Comparable findings have been corroborated in additional publications [17, 20]. Conversely, the findings of our study did not indicate an elevated incidence of post-intravitreal-injection infectious endophthalmitis associated with bevacizumab; indeed, the observed incidence rate of 0.049% was numerically lower than the overall mean for all biologic agents evaluated (0.052%). Similarly, meta-analyses of published literature have reported no increase in post-intravitreal-injection infectious endophthalmitis rates attributable to bevacizumab [19].

Approximately 28% of subjects become nonpersistent with IVT anti-VEGF injections in the first year of therapy and more discontinue therapy after 1 year [49, 50]. Reasons include perceived lack of improvement or visual stabilization, treatment burden (frequent clinic visits and injections), cost or insurance issues, side effects or complications, physician recommendation to monitor rather than continue active therapy, or personal preference [51]. In our study, the cumulative incidence of infectious endophthalmitis was positively correlated with the total number of IVT injections administered. Implementing strategies such as gene therapy that allows long-term, localized production of therapeutic proteins, and other sustained release technologies aimed at reducing IVT injection frequency, has the potential to lower the incidence of infectious endophthalmitis and the consequent risk of significant vision loss [52, 53].

Leveraging the IRIS® Registry, which aggregates longitudinal clinical data from extensive subject populations nationwide through direct extraction from EHRs, enabled analysis at a scale inaccessible by other sources such as administrative claims data. The retrospective, observational design introduces some inherent limitations, yet the registry’s breadth helps address the challenges faced by prospective clinical trials for post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis, namely, the substantial cost and the requirement of large sample sizes. As with other large databases containing individual subject data [54], the IRIS® Registry is subject to limitations, notably its reliance on coded clinical data, which may be subject to imprecision and occasional inaccuracies [55]. Real-world clinical data lacks the rigorous validation characteristic of clinical trial datasets; data are not monitored, nor are queries routinely generated to clinical practices as would occur prior to database lock in a clinical trial setting. Aside from medications identified through J-codes, including the biologic agents analyzed in this study, the availability of detailed data on additional subject medications may be limited. Procedural details such as the anatomical quadrant used for IVT injections, sterility protocols at individual clinical practices, and microbial culture results may also be absent from EHRs. Another notable limitation is incomplete participation by US retinal specialist practices, with nonparticipation prevalent among large private equity groups and academic institutions. This can introduce selection bias since tertiary care academic centers may receive referrals for more severe infectious endophthalmitis cases, which could be underrepresented in our study, affecting its generalizability. Lastly, brolucizumab has been associated with an elevated risk of severe, sterile intraocular inflammation and retinal vascular occlusion [56]. Owing to the absence of comprehensive clinical review for each individual within the IRIS® Registry, there is the possibility that immune-mediated, sterile inflammatory events were incorrectly classified as ‘infectious endophthalmitis.’ This misclassification bias may similarly apply to other drugs evaluated in our study, representing a methodological limitation inherent to large, EHR-based sources such as the IRIS® Registry.

Despite the limitations of the IRIS® Registry, as of 7 September 2025, more than 100 peer-reviewed publications indexed in PubMed have utilized the IRIS® database, solidifying its position as one of the most extensive resources in ophthalmology for characterizing populations, disease epidemiology, clinical outcomes, and complications [10].

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the largest real-world evaluation to date of visual outcomes in subjects who developed infectious endophthalmitis after IVT administration of anti-VEGF or anti-complement agents. In 13,074 subjects (13,317 eyes), the incidence of infectious endophthalmitis per injection was 0.052% and the cumulative rate of infectious endophthalmitis per subject increased from 0.31% after 10 IVT injections to 0.58% after 60 injections. Following the infectious endophthalmitis event, the proportion of subjects classified as legally blind increased by 142% compared with baseline, and those unable to read any letters increased by 260% from pre-event levels. While the incidence of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis was comparable to literature values reported after cataract surgery, visual acuity outcomes are worse in the post-IVT injection group. This difference in visual outcome may also be influenced by the underlying retinal pathology typically present in subjects receiving anti-VEGF therapy rather than procedural differences alone. The drugs with the highest incidence of post-IVT-injection infectious endophthalmitis were brolucizumab (0.101%), aflibercept HD, 8 mg (0.073%), faricimab (0.061%), and aflibercept 2 mg (0.056%). The rates of nonpersistent with anti-VEGF injections with subjects with anti-VEGF therapy are troubling since this results in undertreatment and loss of visual acuity over time. Encouraging subjects to continue frequent injections, however, increases the cumulative risk of developing sight-threatening infectious endophthalmitis. Subjects with RVO and myopic CNV had the highest incidence rates of infectious endophthalmitis. Implementing strategies aimed at reducing IVT injection frequency may have the potential to lower the incidence of infectious endophthalmitis and the consequent risk of significant vision loss.
