Abstract

Purpose: To examine disparities in visual acuity (VA) outcomes 1 year and 2 years after initiation of diabetic retinopathy (DR) or diabetic macular edema (DME) treatment in patients based on race/ethnicity and insurance status, accounting for disease severity. Methods: This retrospective analysis used the IRIS Registry and included DR patients older than 18 years with documented antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) treatment and VA data for at least 2 years. International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes were used to determine the severity of DR and DME presence. VA outcomes were assessed using multivariable linear regressions and anti-VEGF drug use by multivariable logistic regressions, with race and insurance status as independent variables. Main outcome measures comprised the mean VA change at 1 year and 2 years and percentage of patients treated with bevacizumab. Results: The study included 43 274 eyes. White patients presented with a higher mean VA and lower mean DR severity than Black patients and Hispanic patients. Multivariable logistic regression showed Hispanic patients were significantly more likely to be treated with bevacizumab than White patients across all insurance types, controlling for disease severity and VA. After 1 year, the letter improvement was 1.73, 1.33, and 1.13 in White patients, Black patients, and Hispanic patients, respectively. Multivariable linear regression suggested that across races, Medicaid-insured patients had significantly smaller gains in VA than privately insured patients. Conclusions: Race-based and insurance-based differences in 1-year and 2-year outcomes after anti-VEGF treatment for DR and anti-VEGF treatment patterns suggest a need to ensure earlier and more effective treatment of minority and underserved patients in the United States.

Keywords: diabetic retinopathy, antivascular endothelial growth factor, race, health equity, insurance

Introduction

Nationally rising rates of diabetes mellitus (DM) have contributed to an increased incidence of diabetic macular edema (DME) and diabetic retinopathy (DR), 2 of the leading causes of blindness in the industrialized world. 1 Antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy has revolutionized the treatment of DME, with participants in large randomized controlled trials showing approximately 2 lines of visual improvement at 1 year.2 –5 In clinical practice, however, DME patients receive fewer anti-VEGF injections, obtain less visual gains than patients in randomized clinical trials at 1 year, and experience poorer longer term outcomes.6 –14

Patients from racial and ethnic minority groups likely experience even poorer outcomes because clear racial and ethnic disparities exist regarding patient access to, and use of, high-quality DM screening and glycemic control regimens.15 –18 For example, Black patients and Hispanic patients tend to receive lower rates of glycosolated hemoglobin (HbA1c) testing than White patients. 19 Hispanic patients have lower rates of diabetes education and perform less self-monitoring of blood glucose than non-Hispanic White patients. 15 In the United States, the prevalence of DR is higher among Black patients than White patients. 20 At initial treatment for DME, Black patients and Hispanic patients have worse visual acuity (VA), with 1.23 and 1.71 greater odds, respectively, of presenting with worse baseline DR than White patients and non-Hispanic patients. 21

Few studies have used large databases to explore disparities in DR and DME care based on race, ethnicity, and insurance. Previous studies have analyzed racial and socioeconomic differences in the treatment of DR and wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD) with anti-VEGF injections.21 –25 However, these studies did not include specific disease severity metrics or insurance status when evaluating 1-year and 2-year outcomes. In addition, data from the DRCR Retina Protocol T trial showed that at 2 years, patients with a VA of 20/50 or worse had significantly greater improvements in VA after treatment with aflibercept relative to bevacizumab (P = .02). 2

The current study used the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) IRIS (Intelligent Research in Sight) Registry to examine how the choice of treatment and outcomes for patients with DME or DR vary by race, ethnicity, and insurance status.

Methods

Database

This retrospective analysis used the IRIS Registry, an aggregated, de-identified database with more than 454 million patient visits from 75.4 million unique patients since 2013. Approximately 15 920 clinicians in ophthalmology practices from diverse demographic and geographic locations in the US contribute to this database through their electronic health record systems (https://www.aao.org/iris-registry/data-analysis/requirements). These de-identified data were compiled, abstracted, and made available by the AAO through a Hoskins Grant award to one of the authors (R.K.M.).

Eligibility Criteria and Study Design

Between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2018, the database search for DR yielded 2 148 019 unique patients (4 031 143 unique eyes) with a diagnosis of DR, with or without DME. The study included patients diagnosed with DR after the age of 18 years who received anti-VEGF treatment only after their diagnosis.

Patients with other concurrent diagnoses that may require treatment with anti-VEGF agents (eg, AMD, central retinal vein occlusion [CRVO], or choroidal neovascularization) were excluded. Cases were also excluded for unspecified laterality, cataract surgery in the first year of DR treatment, undocumented VA or VA worse than 20/400 (>1.3 logMAR) at baseline, or undocumented VA at the 1-year and 2-year follow-ups. Patients with race/ethnicity listed as “other,” “unknown,” or “Asian” as well as those treated with focal laser and/or an intravitreal steroid in conjunction with anti-VEGF therapies were excluded because the sample size was insufficient. Only 1 eye per patient was included, with laterality randomly selected if both eyes met the inclusion criteria. The final data analysis cohort included 43 274 eyes.

Analysis

Race and ethnicity data were aggregated from the IRIS Registry into 3 available categories as follows: non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic or Latino, subsequently referred to as White, Black, and Hispanic, respectively. Insurance status was divided into Medicare (comprising fee-for-service and advantage programs), Medicaid, private, and other; the latter included military, Indian Health Service, and unknown insurance status. Smoking status consisted of current smoker, former smoker, nonsmoker, and unknown smoking status.

Patients were classified into 1 of 4 intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment categories as follows: off-label bevacizumab (Avastin, Genentech, Inc), ranibizumab (Lucentis, Genentech, Inc), aflibercept (Eylea, Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc), or a combination. If a patient received more than 1 anti-VEGF drug within the first 365 days after their first injection, they were categorized as combination. VA scores were calculated using an Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study approximation with the following formula: 85 + 50 × log(Snellen fraction), as described by Gregori et al. 26

The Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Score (DRSS) scale quantifies the level of disease of patients with retinopathy, ranging from 20 for patients with mild microaneurysms only up to 75 for patients with high-risk proliferative DR (PDR). This scale was modified in the study’s analysis to account for the presence of macular edema. Five points were added to the severity score of patients with macular edema to adjust for the increased disease burden. This adjusted metric can provide a more granular understanding of the treatment outcomes and therapeutic approaches attributed to patients with slightly different disease burdens. However, because only International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), diagnostic codes include both edema status and PDR status in a single diagnostic code (edema status is not present in ICD-9 diagnostic codes), only patients with the requisite ICD-10 codes were included in analyses that involved severity score. This reduced the total number of eyes to 20 787 for all severity-specific analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.1.2, R Foundation) using the tidyverse and lubridate packages. The proportion of patients with 15-letter vision loss (3 or more lines) was used to represent appreciable declines in vision at 1 year and 2 years, per previous studies.27,28 Frequencies and percentages were calculated to examine population-level differences in baseline vision, treatment, and outcomes.

Multivariable regressions used the subset of patients who had ICD-10 diagnostic codes to assess significant differences between racial and insurance categorizations. Multivariable linear regression assessed significant factors associated with changes in VA after 1 year and 2 years, controlling for baseline VA, severity score, anti-VEGF treatment received, sex, age at DR diagnosis, and smoking status. In patients with a baseline VA of 20/50 or worse, multivariable logistic regression was used to assess significant factors associated with the administration of bevacizumab, controlling for baseline VA, severity score, sex, age at DR diagnosis, and smoking status (n = 17 926).

A subsequent multivariable logistic regression included an interaction variable between race and insurance status to assess how insurance type mediated the relationship between race and treatment with bevacizumab. Variance inflation factors were computed to assess for potential collinearity among covariates included in the multivariable model. Significant associations were evaluated at the 0.05 alpha level for all multivariable regressions.

Results

Study Population Demographics, Baseline Vision, and Retinopathy Scores

Based on eligibility criteria, this study included 43 274 eyes. Table 1 shows the patients’ demographic factors. The mean computed VA letter scores at baseline were 67.3, 66.3, and 65.2 for White patients, Black patients, and Hispanic patients, respectively (higher VA score represents better VA). Hispanic patients had lower rates of active smoking than Black patients and White patients and comprised a greater proportion of patients younger than 65 years. Compared with the distribution of treatment by sex across White patients and Hispanic patients, a substantially greater proportion of Black female patients than Black male patients were treated.

Of the patients with available ICD-10 disease severity data (n = 20 787), the mean modified-DRSS scores at baseline (first treatment) were 56.0, 57.3, and 60.7, respectively, in White patients, Black patients, and Hispanic patients (higher DRSS score represents greater severity of DR). The proportion of Hispanic patients classified into higher severity categories was greater than the proportion of Black patients and White patients (P < .001). Figure 1A shows the distribution of baseline DRSS scores by race. Furthermore, for all levels of baseline VA, Hispanic patients presented with more severe DRSS scores than White patients (Figure 1B).

Visual Outcomes After Treatment

At 1 year, White patients had a 1.73-letter improvement vs a 1.33-letter improvement (P = .032) and 1.13-letter (P = .001) improvement in Black patients and Hispanic patients, respectively (Figure 2A). The 1-year improvement in VA was followed by a decline in VA in the second year of treatment across all races and insurance statuses. Only 6.8% of White patients lost at least 15 letters of VA at 1 year compared with 8.7% of Hispanic patients and 7.7% of Black patients (Supplemental Figure 1A). VA outcomes also varied by insurance status. Specifically, Medicaid-insured patients achieved only a 0.91-letter improvement at 1 year compared with a 1.37-letter improvement (P = .092) and 2.16-letter improvement (P < .001) in the Medicare population and private insurance population, respectively (Figure 2B). In addition, 6.4% of privately insured patients had a 15-letter loss at 1 year compared with 7.2% of Medicare-insured patients and 10.0% of Medicaid-insured patients (Supplemental Figure 1B).

Among privately insured patients, White patients achieved 1.25 more letters than Hispanic patients (P = .002) and 0.71 more letters than Black patients (P = .10) at 2 years (Figure 2C). However, in Medicare populations, Black patients achieved the largest 2-year improvement. Although White patients achieved better VA outcomes at 1 year and 2 years than Black patients and Hispanic patients, racial differences in the 1-year and 2-year outcomes were most notable in the privately insured group.

As shown in the multivariable linear regression results (Figure 3), Hispanic patients (−1.28 letters; P < .001) and Black patients (−1.41 letters; P < .001) had significantly less improvement in VA than White patients after 1 year. The additional loss in VA attributed to Medicare or Medicaid coverage compared with private insurance was not significant in Hispanic patients with Medicare or Medicaid. Worse disease severity scores were associated with significantly smaller improvements in VA (−0.05 letters; P < .001). Current smokers also had significantly worse outcomes (−0.57 letters; P = .004) than nonsmokers at 1 year.

Therapy

The proportion of Hispanic patients treated with bevacizumab was greater than the proportion of White patients and Black patients across all disease severities (Figure 4A). The proportion of White patients treated with aflibercept was greater than the proportion of Black patients and Hispanic patients across most disease severities (Supplemental Figure 2A). The proportion of patients with Medicaid treated with bevacizumab was higher vs patients with Medicare and private insurance across all disease severities (Figure 4B), while patients with Medicare or private insurance were treated with aflibercept at higher rates than Medicaid-insured patients across all disease severities (Supplemental Figure 2B).

Multivariable logistic regression showed that Hispanic patients (odds ratio [OR], 2.04; P < .001) and Black patients (OR, 1.37; P < .001) were significantly more likely than White patients to be treated with bevacizumab after controlling for insurance status, baseline VA, and disease severity (Figure 5). Patients with Medicaid (OR, 1.52; P = .02) and those with Medicare (OR, 1.12; P = .005) were significantly more likely than privately insured patients to be treated with bevacizumab.

An interaction variable was used in secondary analyses to assess whether the effect of race on VA changes was mediated by insurance status. Results of this analysis showed that Hispanic patients with private insurance were 218% more likely to be treated with bevacizumab than White patients with private insurance (Supplemental Table 1).

Conclusions

This study found a national disparity in the baseline disease severity, treatment, and outcomes of DR patients by race/ethnicity and by insurance status. Overall, compared with White patients and Black patients, Hispanic patients presented with more severe DR and a lower VA at baseline. Medicaid patients similarly had a worse baseline VA than privately insured patients. Although the reported differences in VA between races and insurance statuses were relatively small, the statistically significant difference between groups could signal a relevant population-level disparity given the large study size. Furthermore, these results are similar to those in a recent study that used IRIS Registry data. 21

The reasons for these disparities in DR and DME care based on race/ethnicity and insurance are likely complex and multifactorial. Lack of access to transportation and primary care providers may delay ophthalmic care, resulting in worse baseline VA. Low income is associated with poorer nutritional intake and food insecurity, leading to higher HbA1c levels and worse baseline DR severity at the onset of treatment.29,30 Furthermore, poorer health literacy has been shown to adversely affect eye health, with lower health literacy documented in ethnic/racial groups, the elderly, populations with lower educational attainment, and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. 31

At 1 year and 2 years, Medicaid-insured patients had a smaller improvement in VA than privately insured patients across all races (Figure 2, B and C; Figure 3). These data suggest that insurance status impacts VA outcomes. The interplay of several factors may contribute to this disparity in outcomes, which disproportionately affects low-income patients.32,33 Access to consistent transportation for follow-up medical appointments, the availability and affordability of foods that help maintain glycemic control, and access to retina specialists may limit patients’ improvements in VA at 1 year and 2 years. This shows there is a need to identify opportunities to improve Medicaid patient care, including providing greater outreach in Medicaid-prevalent populations to attend comprehensive ophthalmic checkups, developing more durable therapies that minimize the need for extensive follow-up, and improving accessibility to specialized ophthalmic care.

A greater proportion of Hispanic patients and Black patients than White patients incurred a 15-letter loss in vision. In addition, minority patients had more severe DR and worse VA at baseline. These results show a national trend toward more severe and uncontrolled disease in minority and low-income populations. Costs (copayments, time away from work) and transportation concerns (access to and cost of transportation, availability of family to accompany patients to appointments) can limit access to adequate follow-up care in these populations. Patient distrust of both physicians and the healthcare system as a product of historical injustices (eg, Tuskegee Syphilis Study) and physician implicit biases may reduce medication adherence and follow-up compliance. Future research assessing racial disparities in VA should continue to analyze the multifaceted underpinnings of this population-level disparity.

Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that the use of bevacizumab was disproportionately higher among Hispanic patients than White patients and Black patients across all disease severities, even after controlling for insurance status, severity score, and baseline severity. These results highlight a potential racial/ethnic disparity in the treatment of DME with anti-VEGF agents that exists nationally, given the results of Protocol T showing a treatment advantage for aflibercept in patients with VA worse than 20/50. 2 Studies report similar racial differences in the administration of anti-VEGF treatments for wet AMD, CRVO, and branch retinal vein occlusion, with Hispanic patients less likely than White patients to initially be treated with ranibizumab or aflibercept. 23 This further supports the notion of physician bias in minority patients being treated with bevacizumab, a trend that may be partially explained by preconceived beliefs regarding the patient having the means to cover the cost of anti-VEGF therapy or copayment. Issues with technological access and literacy may disproportionately limit non-English speakers and poorer patients in communicating their insurance information to their physician. 34 Conscious or unconscious physician biases may also play a role in this disparity. If physicians believe that minority patients are more likely to incorrectly convey their insurance information, they may attempt to avoid costly insurance reimbursement issues (eg, authorization requirements) for aflibercept and ranibizumab by treating patients with bevacizumab.

Insurance policies may also contribute to this disparity in anti-VEGF use. During the study period, some insurers may have implemented step therapy for treatment with anti-VEGF agents, in which bevacizumab must be used as a first-line treatment for DME and DR before the use of aflibercept and ranibizumab. One race being differentially more likely to be enrolled in an insurance plan that requires step therapy could partially explain the differences in use. Although Medicare Advantage plans were unable to initiate step therapy during the study window, certain health maintenance organizations and Medicare administrative contractors may have introduced policies that required step therapy for the treatment of DME and DR with anti-VEGF drugs, increasing the number of patients in the bevacizumab and combination categories. Our insurance data were not granular enough to ascertain whether an individual was enrolled in a plan that required step therapy. Beginning in 2019, after the study period concluded, the US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services allowed Medicare Advantage plans to use step therapy. 35 Thus, in the future, insurance policies may continue to skew the use of bevacizumab by race.

In addition, differences in access to or use of diabetes screening, cultural differences in diet or attitudes toward diabetes control, and access to a primary care physician and/or ophthalmologist could collectively contribute to a baseline disparity in VA and disease severity by race and socioeconomic status and to poorer visual outcomes and treatment disparities. Efforts to educate physicians on potential racial biases in care delivery for DR may serve to limit these racial disparities in care nationally.

Multivariable logistic regression also found that Medicare and Medicaid patients were significantly more likely than privately insured patients to receive bevacizumab injections. We posit multiple reasons for this insurance-based disparity in care provision. If a provider believes that Medicare-insured patients will be unable to submit their copayment for aflibercept, the provider might choose to administer bevacizumab, a much less expensive alternative to aflibercept and ranibizumab.

This study is limited by its retrospective design without anatomic data or controls, drop-off in follow-up over time, and nonstandardized VA assessment. Furthermore, data on the number of anti-VEGF injections received by each patient were not available to include in the analyses, limiting the robustness of the results. The dataset lacked sociodemographic variables to fully represent individual and community-level inequities, including census block, ZIP code, and language status. The analysis was unable to distinguish race and ethnicity, so patients who identified racially as White and ethnically as Hispanic were automatically classified as Hispanic. The insurance variable lacked granularity on insurance coverage to explore specific copayment rates for each anti-VEGF treatment. However, the large sample is a strength of this work. This analysis yields relevant insights on race-based and insurance-based differences in DR treatment and outcomes.

In conclusion, this study found a disparity in the baseline DR severity, treatment, and outcomes by race and insurance status. Black and Hispanic patients achieved significantly worse outcomes after 1 year and 2 years than White patients, and Hispanic patients were disproportionately treated with bevacizumab after controlling for insurance and disease severity. Similarly, Medicaid-insured patients had lower VA at baseline, achieved significantly lower improvements in VA after 1 year across races, and were treated with bevacizumab in greater proportions relative to privately insured patients. Understanding disparities in DR visual outcomes can lead to improvements in care for this increasingly prevalent disease.
