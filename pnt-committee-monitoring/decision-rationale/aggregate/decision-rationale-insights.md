## General Decision-Making Patterns

1. Public rationale is usually clinical and criteria-oriented. The most visible rationale layer is safety, efficacy, effectiveness, diagnosis, age, lab or monitoring requirements, contraindications, previous therapy, continuation criteria, and medical necessity.

2. Access impact is often expressed through implementation artifacts, not deliberation text. The highest-value outputs are preferred/non-preferred status, prior authorization criteria, step therapy, quantity limits, effective dates, provider bulletins, final PDL files, and criteria updates.

3. Recommendations and final decisions are frequently separate. Many committees advise an agency director, commissioner, secretary, chief medical officer, or Medicaid program office. Pharma monitoring should track the full chain: agenda, packet, public comment, recommendation/minutes, agency decision, implementation bulletin, and effective PDL/PA criteria.

4. Cost and rebate logic matter but are unevenly public. Cost-effectiveness, net cost, supplemental rebates, and budget impact appear in some sources, with richer evidence in Oklahoma and state reports such as Vermont. In many states, rebate and net-cost details are confidential or absent, so pharma should record economic opacity as a competitive-intelligence finding rather than infer undisclosed rationale.

5. Manufacturer and stakeholder engagement windows are state-specific. Useful windows include manufacturer submission deadlines, public testimony registration, written comment periods, speaker forms, stakeholder input sections, and restrictions on direct contact with committee members.

6. Criteria edits can be more actionable than PDL status alone. Virginia and South Dakota show that products may be approved, tabled, or modified based on indication wording, definitions, baseline requirements, renewal criteria, safety monitoring, or preferred-product trials.

7. DUR boards, PDL advisory committees, PA advisory committees, and drug formulary committees often function as P&T equivalents. A pharma monitoring program should follow functional decision bodies rather than only committees literally named P&T.

8. Documentation burden is a recurring access lever. Tennessee, West Virginia, Wisconsin, and Wyoming show practical distinctions between chart-note evidence, laboratory values, diagnosis confirmation, provider attestation, prior therapy history, and renewal documentation.

## Therapy And Disease-Area Patterns

### Diabetes, GLP-1, And Obesity

GLP-1, diabetes, insulin, obesity, weight-loss, semaglutide, tirzepatide, Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound, and related terms recur across the corpus. The strongest themes are diagnosis-specific PA, off-label weight-loss concern, non-covered weight-loss benefit constraints, high spend/utilization monitoring, and differentiation between diabetes treatment and obesity indications.

High-value implication: teams should prepare indication-specific evidence, coding support, diagnosis criteria, off-label-use mitigation, continuation criteria, and Medicaid benefit-status arguments separately for diabetes, obesity, OSA, MASH, and related metabolic indications. Tennessee and Wyoming provide especially useful examples of BMI/comorbidity cutoffs, lifestyle participation, duplicate GLP-1 restrictions, renewal thresholds, and cost-analysis referrals.

### Immunology, Dermatology, Biologics, And Biosimilars

Humira/adalimumab, ustekinumab/Stelara, Dupixent, Skyrizi, Rinvoq, Cosentyx, Taltz, Xolair, biologic, biosimilar, psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn disease, and ulcerative colitis appear in PDL, minutes, agenda, and criteria contexts.

High-value implication: expect preference logic around biosimilar availability, class sequencing, trial-before-access requirements, and clinical equivalence. Evidence should distinguish route, indication breadth, pediatric status, prior biologic failure, and continuation response.

### Oncology, Rare Disease, And High-Cost Specialty

Oncology, cancer, antineoplastic, hemophilia, gene therapy, cell therapy, cystic fibrosis, sickle cell, orphan, and named oncology or rare-disease products appear in several groups. Virginia provides the strongest product-level new-drug criteria-review evidence in this pass, including examples of approved, edited, and tabled criteria.

High-value implication: pharma should monitor new-drug and pipeline-review sections, not just established PDL classes. Oncology and rare-disease evidence packages should be indication-specific and should anticipate criteria around FDA label wording, line of therapy, testing, biomarkers, monitoring, duration, and specialist involvement.

Wyoming and Virginia show a particularly important pattern for rare disease and specialty products: committees may approve or limit access to FDA indication while separately referring the product for cost analysis or future review when comparative evidence is limited, approval is accelerated, populations are small, or head-to-head evidence is pending.

### Behavioral Health, CNS, ADHD, Migraine, And Alzheimer Disease

ADHD/stimulants, antipsychotics, antidepressants, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, Alzheimer agents, migraine, CGRP, seizure, and epilepsy terms are frequent. Missouri, Michigan, Mississippi, South Dakota, Washington, and Group D states show these classes appearing in PDL edits, proposals, agendas, criteria, or minutes.

High-value implication: monitor criteria around diagnosis confirmation, age, prior therapy, misuse/diversion risk, quantity limits, continuation, and alternative preferred agents. For migraine/CGRP classes, expect acute versus preventive distinctions and step requirements.

Wisconsin adds a useful exception framework for several CNS and behavioral-health classes, while West Virginia and Tennessee show active review of antipsychotics, antidepressants/SNRIs, stimulants, ADHD/narcolepsy agents, and migraine therapies through criteria or PDL-change artifacts.

### OUD, Opioids, Pain, And Overdose

Opioid, buprenorphine, naloxone, Suboxone, Sublocade, methadone, non-opioid alternatives, pain, and utilization-control language appear across several groups. Tennessee's audit and multiple state criteria/PDL sources show opioid strategy as a public-health and utilization-management issue.

High-value implication: coverage positioning should separate pain management, OUD treatment, overdose reversal, withdrawal management, and abuse-deterrent or quantity-limit logic. The rationale is often population-safety and utilization-driven rather than only product comparative evidence.

### Respiratory, Allergy, And Cystic Fibrosis

Asthma, COPD, inhalers, allergy agents, cystic fibrosis, Alyftrek, Xolair, inhaled antibiotics, and related terms appear in Michigan, Missouri, Ohio, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Washington, and other states.

High-value implication: monitor class review schedules and criteria updates for prior controller therapy, exacerbation history, pulmonary function, phenotype or mutation requirements, specialist prescribing, and continuation outcomes.

### Infectious Disease, Vaccines, HIV, And Hepatitis

HIV, HCV/hepatitis C, hepatitis B, anti-infectives, antivirals, influenza, RSV, COVID-19, vaccines, PrEP, and antiretroviral terms appear across the corpus.

High-value implication: evidence planning should separate public-health coverage rationale, federal guideline alignment, resistance or genotype requirements, vaccine policy, and Medicaid pharmacy versus medical benefit routing.

### Cardiovascular, Renal, Hematology, And Metabolic Specialty

Anticoagulants, PAH, ATTR-CM, heart failure, lipid/PCSK9, hemophilia, thrombocytopenia, renal, endocrine, and hematology terms appear in Group D and Group E sources.

High-value implication: criteria often turn on diagnosis precision, genotype or biomarker status, specialist confirmation, prior therapy, contraindications, and high-cost product management.

## High-Value Pharma Operating Model

- Build a per-state source watchlist that includes agendas, packets, minutes, recommendation PDFs, final decision PDFs, PDL files, PA criteria, provider notices, bulletins, public-comment instructions, manufacturer submission policies, and effective-date notices.
- Normalize each source into fields: state, body, date, product/class, disease area, action type, recommendation, final authority, rationale text, criteria changes, effective date, manufacturer/public-comment window, source URL, and evidence-gap flags.
- Treat agenda mentions as early-warning signals, not outcomes.
- Treat PDL/PA/criteria changes as the practical access impact.
- Track tabled decisions and criteria edits as high-value signals because they reveal what evidence or wording was insufficient.
- Separate clinical rationale from economic opacity. When cost/rebate logic is absent, mark it as a gap rather than infer undisclosed pricing rationale.
- Calendar manufacturer-material deadlines separately from public meeting dates. Wyoming's seven-day written-material requirement, Wisconsin's structured testimony rules, and Tennessee's five-minute testimony structure show that engagement windows can close before the committee date.
- Prioritize richer states for repeatable monitoring models: Oklahoma, Virginia, South Dakota, Washington, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, North Carolina, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Tennessee, Wisconsin, Wyoming, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, and Vermont's published cost-control report.
- Treat blocked/failed sources as explicit residual risk. Massachusetts, blocked Texas direct decision PDFs, blocked Vermont DURB materials, and several obsolete/404 pages remain source-access issues rather than capped-crawl gaps.
