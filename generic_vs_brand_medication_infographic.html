Generic drugs dominate prescriptions but “Americans only” needs a caveat

Current national sources support a prescription-fill split. The clean public person-level split for “only generic” versus “only brand-name” is older MEPS evidence, so it should not be presented as current.

9 in 10 U.S. prescriptions are filled as generic or biosimilar medicines

AAM/IQVIA report 3.9B generic fills versus 435M brand fills in 2024.1 FDA separately summarizes the U.S. market as 9 out of 10 prescriptions filled for generics.2

Derived estimate, not a directly published cross-tab: AHRQ reported 53.4% of the community population purchased a brand-name medicine and 46.0% purchased a generic medicine in 2003; 64.4% had any outpatient prescription drug expense. The implied overlap is 35.0 points, leaving 11.0 points generic-only and 18.4 points brand-only in the full population; dividing by 64.4 gives the user-level split shown here.

Most adults are in the prescription market

66%

KFF polling in March 2026 found that two-thirds of U.S. adults currently take at least one prescription drug.5

Generic volume does not mean generic spending

12%

Generic and biosimilar medicines represented 90% of fills but only 12% of prescription drug spending in 2024.1

Regulatory meaning of “generic”

matched

FDA says approved generics match brand-name medicines in active ingredient, dosage form, strength, route, quality, performance, and intended use; inactive ingredients or appearance may differ.6

Bottom line

If the claim is about today’s medication use, say about 90% of prescriptions filled are generic or biosimilar. If the claim is about Americans using only generics versus only brand-name drugs, label it as an older person-level MEPS estimate rather than a current national fact.
