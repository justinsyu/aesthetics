# MPS II / Hunter Syndrome Competitive Intelligence Website Search Plan

Prepared: May 11, 2026

Scope: This is a competitive intelligence source and monitoring plan for mucopolysaccharidosis type II (MPS II), also known as Hunter syndrome. It identifies credible websites to search and monitor for clinical, regulatory, pipeline, market access, and commercial intelligence. It is not a CI findings report.

## CI objectives

- Track changes in the MPS II treatment landscape, including standard intravenous enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), blood-brain-barrier-penetrant ERT, intrathecal or intracerebroventricular ERT, AAV gene therapy, ex vivo hematopoietic stem-cell gene therapy, newborn-screening-enabled earlier treatment, and supportive care.
- Detect competitor and development signals early: trial starts, protocol amendments, enrollment updates, clinical holds, complete response letters, accelerated approval commitments, label changes, expanded access or named-patient programs, publication plans, abstracts, posters, investor disclosures, and partnership/licensing changes.
- Monitor evidence-generation infrastructure: Hunter Outcome Survey (HOS), natural-history studies, newborn screening evidence reviews, biomarkers such as cerebrospinal fluid heparan sulfate and urinary glycosaminoglycans, neurodevelopmental endpoints, caregiver burden, quality of life, and real-world treatment patterns.
- Track regulatory and access signals that affect commercial opportunity: FDA/EMA/PMDA/Health Canada/MHRA status, orphan and rare pediatric disease designations, confirmatory trial requirements, payer prior authorization criteria, specialty infusion logistics, home infusion policies, coding/payment updates, patient support programs, and HTA or reimbursement decisions.
- Maintain an auditable search workflow that starts with official and primary sources, uses peer-reviewed journals and recognized disease organizations for context, and treats media, social, and investor commentary as leads requiring primary-source validation.

## Source-quality notes

- Treat official regulatory databases and labels as the highest-confidence sources for approved indication, dosing, safety warnings, exclusivity, and postmarketing requirements: FDA, EMA, PMDA, Health Canada, MHRA, TGA, DailyMed.
- Treat ClinicalTrials.gov, CTIS, EudraCT, WHO ICTRP, and ISRCTN as authoritative for public trial-record fields, but confirm major program interpretation against sponsor disclosures, publications, and regulatory documents.
- Company press releases, investor decks, and SEC filings are primary sponsor sources, but they are not neutral. Use them for timing, claims, trial updates, financing, and commercial intent; validate efficacy/safety claims against labels, trial registries, manuscripts, and regulatory review documents.
- Patient societies and foundations are highly valuable for stakeholder priorities, advocacy, education, access friction, newborn-screening policy, and trial-recruitment context. Do not use them as sole sources for clinical efficacy or regulatory status.
- Peer-reviewed journals are preferred for clinical efficacy, safety, natural history, registry outcomes, and burden data. Check funding, sponsor involvement, endpoint definitions, population comparability, and whether the article is primary research, review, conference abstract, or commentary.
- Payer policies and HTA documents are credible for access criteria and evidence interpretation by payers, but they may be outdated, plan-specific, state-specific, or not applicable to all lines of business. Capture publication/effective dates and plan type.
- Conference abstracts and posters are often early and incomplete. Record abstract date, sponsor, data cutoff, and whether the result has been peer-reviewed.
- FAERS and other spontaneous adverse-event databases are signal-generation tools only. They cannot estimate incidence or prove causality without further analysis.
- Subscription sources such as MMIT, IPD Analytics, Evaluate, Clarivate, Citeline, First Databank, and IQVIA can support access and market analytics, but any key claim should be triangulated with public payer policies, labels, regulatory documents, or company filings when possible.
- Avoid relying on Wikipedia, Reddit, social media, scraped drug pages, or general health sites for CI conclusions. Use them only as lead-generation sources that point back to primary documents.
