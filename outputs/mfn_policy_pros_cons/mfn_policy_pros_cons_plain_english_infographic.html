MFN drug pricing policy

MFN drug pricing policy: pros and cons

Policy definition: MFN refers to using prices in wealthy peer countries as a benchmark so U.S. prices move closer to those paid abroad for the same branded drugs. The current HHS target is the lowest price in an OECD country with GDP per person at least 60% of the U.S. level for brand products without generic or biosimilar competition.[1][2]

Evidence summary

The sections below separate potential benefits from implementation risks. MFN may lower prices where U.S. payments are substantially higher, while outcomes depend on enforceable rules, net-price data, and safeguards for access to medicines.[3][7][8]

U.S. prices vs 33 OECD countries

278%

U.S. manufacturer gross prices across all prescription drugs in 2022, where peer-country prices equal 100%.[3]

Brand-originator gap

422%

U.S. gross prices for branded originator drugs; RAND/ASPE reported that adjusted U.S. brand prices remained more than 3x higher.[3]

Affordability pressure

43%

Share of U.S. adults reporting cost-related medication nonadherence in a 2026 KFF poll.[4]

Potential benefits

Policy rationale

Targets a documented price gap

MFN focuses on branded drugs where the U.S. gap is largest: RAND/ASPE found U.S. brand-originator prices were 422% of prices in 33 OECD comparison countries in 2022.[3]

May lower some patient bills

Patient costs are relevant because 59% of adults reported concern about affording prescriptions and 43% reported skipping or changing medicines because of cost; the White House projects large savings for some cash-pay GLP-1 and IVF users.[4][10]

May reduce public spending

CMS states that its proposed GLOBE model is intended to reduce Medicare Part B spending; the White House projects $529B in 10-year domestic savings and $64.3B in federal and state Medicaid savings under its MFN framework.[5][10]

Provides an external benchmark

Rather than using U.S. list prices alone, policymakers can use peer-country prices as a ceiling, negotiation input, or transparency tool.[9]

Potential limitations

Implementation risks

Coverage may remain unclear

KFF notes that many MFN agreement details remain confidential, including which drugs are covered, the exact prices, and how MFN prices are determined.[6]

Manufacturer responses may reduce savings

Reference-pricing savings may erode if companies raise public list prices, use confidential rebates, or delay launches in lower-price countries so those prices are not used as U.S. benchmarks.[7][9]

Access effects require monitoring

The 2020 MFN rule stated that if providers cannot obtain drugs at the lower price, patients could need different providers, alternate medicines, delayed care, or no treatment; that earlier model was never implemented.[11]

Future R&D incentives may be affected

CBO describes a policy balance: lower brand prices can improve access today, but lower expected global profits can reduce incentives to develop some future drugs.[8]

How strong is the evidence?

Strong

U.S. branded drug prices are much higher than peer-country prices, and prescription costs are associated with reported affordability barriers for many adults.[3][4]

Medium

Reference pricing can reduce spending when it has clear authority, good data, and enforcement; CMS and CBO describe savings pathways, but results depend on design.[5][8]

Uncertain

The exact savings, covered drugs, foreign-price response, access effects, and innovation impact remain uncertain under voluntary or partly confidential MFN arrangements.[6][7]

TAKEAWAY

MFN is most likely to produce predictable results when it is transparent, applies to drugs with limited competition, uses net-price evidence, and includes access protections. Results are less certain when arrangements are confidential, policy claims are broad, or manufacturers can avoid benchmark prices.[6][7][9]
