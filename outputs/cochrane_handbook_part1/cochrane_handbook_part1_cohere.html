Part 1 / Cochrane reviews

A Cochrane review is a protocol-driven synthesis for health and social care decisions.

Chapter I defines systematic reviews as explicit, systematic, protocol-documented work that collates evidence for a specific question while minimizing bias.

What makes a Cochrane review distinct

Pre-specified eligibility criteria are used to answer a specific research question.
Explicit, systematic methods are documented in advance in a protocol to minimize bias.
Cochrane Methods Groups and the Methods Executive support the development and adoption of review methods.
Cochrane reviews are prepared, maintained and promoted to inform health and social care decisions.

Principles guide review practice

Collaboration, avoiding duplication, minimizing bias, keeping up-to-date, relevance, access, quality, continuity, and wide participation shape how Cochrane works.

From intent to protocol

Planning a Cochrane review requires proposal, protocol, team, resources, and conflict checks.

Chapter II covers the additional organizational and practical considerations for author teams preparing a Cochrane review.

Chapter II
Planning a Cochrane review
01 Check novelty

Search the Cochrane Library and avoid duplicating an existing or overlapping review.

02 Submit proposal

Topic experts assess whether the proposed scope is appropriate and avoids duplication.

03 Build the team

Combine topic and methods expertise with stakeholder perspectives and needed specialist input.

04 Write protocol

Specify scope, outcomes, search, data collection, analysis, consumer involvement, and deviations handling.

05 Plan delivery

Budget time, tools, software, data access, search support, analysis support, and future updating.

Protocol as bias control

Cochrane reviews must have written protocols.
Changes to planned methods should be documented and reported in the completed review.
Sensitivity analyses should test deviations when feasible.
PROSPERO registration is recommended to support transparency and reduce duplication.

Authorship follows ICMJE criteria

Named authors should satisfy ICMJE criteria and list specific contributions.
Information specialists and statisticians belong in the byline when they meet authorship criteria.
Cochrane reviews cannot be funded or conducted by commercial sources with a real or potential vested interest in the findings.

Software is part of the plan

RevMan is the mandatory production tool for Cochrane authors.
Covidence, EPPI-Reviewer, reference managers, R, Stata, GRADEpro GDT, and Screen4Me can support specific stages.
New technologies should be assessed for cost, internet needs, stability, and validation before use.

Transparent reporting

Clear reporting lets readers assess methods, interpret findings, and use the review.

Chapter III aligns Cochrane protocols and reviews with PRISMA 2020, PRISMA for Protocols, plain language summaries, and consistency across all summary formats.

Chapter III
Reporting the review

Core reporting elements

Protocols are public records of the question and intended methods before study results are fully known.
Completed reviews should be succinct, readable, and accessible to healthcare decision makers, consumers and policy makers.
Abstracts should stand alone and include key methods, results, certainty, conclusions, funding and registration information.
Plain language summaries should communicate the review question and findings in terms accessible to non-expert readers.
Summary of findings tables should present important comparisons, patient-important outcomes, effects and GRADE certainty.

Conduct and reporting are separate

MECIR guides conduct; PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA for Protocols guide reporting. Poor reporting can obscure what was done and what was found.

Consistency is essential

Objectives, outcomes, results, caveats and conclusions should be consistent across the main text, abstract, plain language summary, tables, figures, and Summary of findings tables.

Section
What must be clear
Common failure to avoid
Methods
Eligibility criteria, sources, search dates, study selection, data collection, risk of bias, synthesis decisions, and deviations.
Vague methods that cannot be replicated or checked against the protocol.
Results
Included studies, participants, interventions, outcomes, missing data, effect direction, estimates, uncertainty, subgroup and sensitivity analyses.
Overstating nonsignificant findings as proof of no effect.
Discussion
Applicability, evidence limitations, review-process limitations, and agreement or disagreement with other reviews.
Repeating results without explaining what constrains interpretation.
Conclusions
Practical implications and research implications supported only by systematically compiled evidence.
Writing recommendations as if the review were a clinical guideline.
Information
Contributions, conflicts, funding, protocol/registration, and data, code, and materials availability.
Leaving users unable to audit provenance or reproduce analyses.

Currency and change control

Update when the question remains important and new information could affect findings.

Chapter IV treats updating as a structured judgement based on continuing relevance, newly available data, and methodological developments.

Chapter IV
Updating a review
Assess Still relevant?

Check whether the review question still matters to current policy, practice, and users.

Audit Still adequate?

Compare the existing question and methods with current standards and recommended approaches.

Scan New signals?

Look for new studies, completed ongoing trials, new data, corrections, retractions, or new methods.

Judge Likely impact?

Ask whether the new evidence or methods could meaningfully affect review findings.

Publish Report changes

Integrate results, revise GRADE assessments when needed, and explain what changed.

Planning an update

Reconsider PICO, eligibility criteria, comparisons, outcomes, and stakeholder priorities.
Split large reviews or merge narrow ones when that better serves decisions.
Consult an information specialist because databases, controlled vocabulary, and terms may have changed.
Consider a complete, updated protocol when scope or methods change substantially.

Conducting an update

The search date should be within 12 months of publication of the update.
New studies require eligibility assessment, data collection, risk-of-bias assessment, synthesis, and revised certainty assessment.
Previously included studies must be checked for new data, corrections, retractions, or problematic-study signals.

Reporting an update

Signal clearly that this is an update in the abstract, background, protocol, and registration sections.
Choose integrated, incremental, or replacement reporting for updated searches and PRISMA flow diagrams.
Use “What’s new” to summarize new studies, participants, certainty changes, conclusion changes, and implications for practice.

Reviews of reviews

Cochrane Overviews summarize evidence across multiple systematic reviews.

Chapter V defines when the unit of searching, inclusion and data analysis should be the systematic review rather than the primary study.

Chapter V
Overviews of Reviews

Five core components of a Cochrane Overview

A clearly formulated objective, usually about healthcare interventions.
An intent to include systematic reviews, with or without meta-analyses.
Explicit methods to identify, include, and appraise multiple reviews.
Extraction and presentation of review characteristics, primary-study risk of bias, outcome data, and GRADE certainty.
Discussion of completeness, applicability, quality, potential biases, and agreement with other evidence.
Unit
Systematic reviews, not individual primary studies.
Use
Broad questions spanning related interventions, populations, outcomes, or conditions.
Issues
Overlapping primary studies, heterogeneous reviews, incomplete GRADE assessments, and missing outcome data.
Not
Primary-study searches, new reviews within the Overview, narrative reviews, or network meta-analysis.

Scope
The question spans multiple related systematic reviews and decision makers need the breadth summarized.
Relevant primary studies are not already captured in reviews or must be extracted directly.
Analysis
Outcome data can be used as reported or re-analysed from included systematic reviews.
Analyses require standardized study-level data or network meta-analysis.
Methods
The team is prepared to manage review overlap, review quality, primary-study risk of bias, and GRADE limitations.
The review evidence is too outdated, too heterogeneous, too incomplete, or too thin to support a useful overview.
