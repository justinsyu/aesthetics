Access to a medical product—-whether a prescription drug, vaccine, or other clinic-administered therapy—is

often shaped less by the product itself and more by the health system around it. Three major drivers are
insurance coverage, clinic availability, and administration logistics.
Insurance coverage determines whether patients can afford the product in the first place. In systems like the
U.S., coverage rules under public programs such as Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services and private
insurers define whether a product is "on formulary," what tier it falls under, and how much out-of-pocket
is a key pillar of universal health coverage because cost-sharing and insurance gaps directly reduce access to
essential medicines and services.
Clinic availability affects whether patients can physically receive the product.If administration requires a
licensed provider (for example, injections or infusions), access depends on how many clinics offer it, whether
they have trained staff, and appointment capacity. In underserved areas, long travel distances or provider
shortages can create “geographic barriers," even when insurance coverage exists. WHO research on service
delivery highlights that workforce distribution and facility density are major determinants of whether care is
practically accessible, not just theoretically available.
Administration logistics can be the hidden bottleneck. Some products require cold-chain storage, infusion
chairs, observation periods after dosing, or specific handling protocols. These requirements can limit which
facilities are authorized to administer the product. Scheduling complexity also matters: if a therapy requires
multiple visits, coordination failures or long wait times can reduce completion rates. In practice, logistical
constraints often create a gap between prescription and actual receipt of treatment, even in well-insured
populations.
Taken together, these three factors interact: insurance determines affordability, clinic availability determines
physical access, and logistics determine whether delivery is operationally feasible once a patient reaches the
system. When any one of these breaks down, access to the product can be significantly reduced—even if it is
clinically appropriate and officially available.
