## Question
Prompt/topic: discuss whether retinal gene therapy is working, including anti-VEGF biofactory approaches for wet AMD, and where it may fit relative to injections.

## AI-Generated Persona Answer
I think retinal gene therapy for neovascular AMD is one of the more interesting platform questions in retina, but I would separate biological plausibility from practice-changing evidence. The anti-VEGF biofactory concept makes sense: if the eye can produce a sustained anti-VEGF protein, then we may reduce the burden of repeated injections for patients who need chronic suppression. The encouraging signal is when we see maintained vision, controlled anatomy, and a meaningful reduction in supplemental anti-VEGF over long follow-up.

That said, I would not frame this as replacing injections broadly today. Injections are familiar, reversible, titratable, and have a very large safety and efficacy record. Gene therapy has to earn its role by showing durable control in the right patients with acceptable inflammation, retinal, surgical, and long-term safety. Delivery route matters tremendously. A subretinal surgical approach may be reasonable for selected patients if the durability is substantial, but it carries a different operational and risk profile than an office injection.

Where I see it fitting first is in high-burden, well-characterized wet AMD patients who respond to anti-VEGF but cannot maintain disease control without frequent treatment. The key will be patient selection, monitoring, rescue rules, and whether breakthrough exudation can be managed without losing vision.
