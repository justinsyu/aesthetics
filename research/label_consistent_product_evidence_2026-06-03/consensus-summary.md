# Consensus Summary: Communicating Product Evidence Consistent With Product Labeling

Research date: 2026-06-03

## Executive Consensus

The consensus across FDA/OPDP, FTC, AMCP, ISPOR, PhRMA, HEOR vendors, and medical communications sources is that product evidence should be presented from a reviewed "evidence platform" rather than as isolated claims. The platform should separate what is directly in the FDA-required labeling from supplementary evidence that may be consistent with the approved use, then tailor the expression of that evidence to the audience: payers/HCDMs, HCPs, patients/consumers, or internal teams.

The core standard is: stay within the approved indication and conditions of use; substantiate every express and implied claim; present study design, population, comparator, endpoints, limitations, uncertainty, and generalizability close to the claim; and avoid implying broader approval, superior safety/effectiveness, clinical utility, or off-label use unless the cited source directly supports that exact message.

## Practical Best Practices

1. Anchor every claim to the product label first.
   - Record the approved indication, population, dosing/use regimen, route, limitations of use, safety context, and any label data relevant to the claim.
   - Distinguish direct label-supported claims from supplementary label-consistent evidence, such as postmarketing data, RWE, subgroup analyses, adherence data, onset data, health resource utilization, or economic evidence.

2. Use audience-specific evidence packages.
   - Payers/HCDMs: emphasize population-level clinical, economic, budget impact, HCRU, utilization, model assumptions, sensitivity analyses, and generalizability.
   - HCPs: emphasize patient-care relevance, study validity, benefits, risks, limitations, and whether information is promotional, medical/scientific, or SIUU.
   - Patients/consumers: simplify numeracy, use absolute risks/frequencies where possible, and ensure risk information is clear and balanced.
   - Social/KOL/influencer contexts: assess implied claims and disclose material connections.

3. Make limitations prominent and proximate.
   - Do not bury limitations in footnotes when they materially qualify a headline or chart.
   - For RWE, observational studies, post hoc analyses, exploratory endpoints, or economic models, state the design, assumptions, limitations, and uncertainty next to the claim or visual that depends on them.
   - Disclosures do not rescue an otherwise unsupported, overstated, or misleading claim.

4. Avoid evidence overreach.
   - Do not present RWE or observational evidence as if it proves causal treatment effects unless the design supports that inference.
   - Do not imply superiority, drug-of-choice status, broader population applicability, improved safety/effectiveness, or unapproved outcomes unless the exact comparison and conclusion are supported.
   - Do not let charts, icons, ordering, colors, testimonials, or before/after imagery imply claims stronger than the text.

5. Present quantitative evidence in decision-useful form.
   - Include control-group or comparator data when relevant.
   - Prefer absolute frequencies or percentages for consumer-facing probability statements.
   - If using relative risk reductions, present the corresponding absolute values with comparable prominence.
   - Use consistent denominators and numerical formats.

6. Maintain a living evidence platform.
   - Keep one reviewed source of truth for claims, studies, model inputs, assumptions, and approved variants.
   - Version control claims and local adaptations.
   - Use evidence gap assessments and update plans so old or local materials do not drift away from the current label, current evidence base, or local payer requirements.

## Typical Disclaimer / Limitation Elements

Use the exact language required by the company, jurisdiction, and MLR process. Common elements to include, when applicable:

- Approved indication and limitations of use.
- Current prescribing information or FDA-approved labeling reference.
- Statement that an unapproved product/use is not approved and safety/effectiveness has not been established.
- For economic or RWE analyses: data source, study design, population, comparator, endpoints, assumptions, model perspective, time horizon, uncertainty/sensitivity analysis, and generalizability limits.
- For exploratory, descriptive, post hoc, or underpowered analyses: state that findings are exploratory/descriptive, should be interpreted cautiously, and may be due to chance.
- For material differences from labeling in payer/HCEI contexts: a conspicuous statement describing those differences.

## High-Risk Pitfalls

- Headline claims that omit material study limitations.
- RWE framed like randomized head-to-head evidence.
- Selective presentation of favorable endpoints while omitting unfavorable or inconsistent findings.
- Subgroup, post hoc, or registry results promoted as established effects.
- Economic models without assumptions, sensitivity analyses, or local context.
- Local claims not aligned to the local label, comparator, practice pattern, or reimbursement context.
- Visuals or testimonials implying unsubstantiated benefit, typicality, superiority, safety, or broader use.
- Mixing approved-use promotion with unapproved-use scientific information in a way that conflates approval status.

## Working Takeaway

The optimal presentation is a transparent, label-anchored evidence narrative: start with approved use and label context, add only fit-for-purpose evidence relevant to the audience's decision, show the methods and limitations where the evidence is consumed, and make the net impression no broader than the data and label support.
