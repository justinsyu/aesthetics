Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

  1. Distinguish nonpromotional scientific exchange from promotional activity and apply fair-balance and substantiation principles. (F)
  2. Explain, at a working level, the legal and ethical frameworks that govern industry interactions: the US Anti-Kickback Statute and False Claims Act, the Physician Payments Sunshine Act, off-label communication rules, the PhRMA Code, and good-practice (GxP) quality systems. (F)
  3. Describe how medical, legal, and regulatory (MLR) review works and the role of the medical reviewer. (F)
  4. Apply data-privacy and confidentiality requirements (for example HIPAA and GDPR) at a working level to medical and evidence activities. (F)

Why this matters for the career changer

A clinician is used to a professional duty of care and to institutional research ethics, but the industry compliance regime is different in kind. It governs not only whether a statement is true but how, to whom, by which function, and in response to what it may be communicated. The same accurate scientific statement can be permissible from a medical reviewer in response to an unsolicited request and impermissible as a proactive promotional message. Many of the most significant enforcement actions in the industry have turned on this distinction rather than on falsehood.

A new professional who cannot classify an activity as promotional, nonpromotional, or requiring review is a compliance risk regardless of scientific skill. This module builds the classification skill and the vocabulary of the compliance boundary. It underpins the whole program's compliance stance, and it is the foundation on which the AI-use policy in Module 14 is built.

Note on scope: this module teaches principles, not legal advice. It describes the frameworks at the level a medical or evidence professional needs in order to recognize and route an issue; it does not substitute for counsel, and it deliberately avoids reciting specific statutory section numbers.

3.1 The nonpromotional boundary

The central concept of the module is the boundary between promotion and nonpromotional scientific exchange.

Promotion advances the use of a product. It is subject to the requirements that govern advertising and labeling, including that claims be truthful, not misleading, consistent with the approved label, and adequately substantiated, and that risk information be presented with a fair balance against benefit claims; in the US these requirements are overseen by the FDA Office of Prescription Drug Promotion (FDA, Office of Prescription Drug Promotion). Communicating a use that is not in the approved label (off-label) through promotional channels is generally prohibited, although defined nonpromotional and scientific channels exist (FDA, Responding to Unsolicited Requests for Off-Label Information About Prescription Drugs and Medical Devices).

Nonpromotional scientific exchange is the objective communication of scientific and medical information. It is the domain of medical affairs. It is not directed at advancing use, it presents evidence in balanced scientific context, and it may address scientific questions that touch on unapproved uses when the exchange is genuinely nonpromotional and appropriately triggered, for example by an unsolicited request. The nonpromotional standard is not merely an internal preference; scientific bodies enforce it. ISPOR, for example, requires that abstracts and workshop submissions use generic technology names and avoid promotional language, and it publishes worked examples that demonstrate the standard (ISPOR, HEOR impact-case abstract example; ISPOR, workshop proposal example).

The practical test a professional applies to any planned communication has three parts:

  1. Intent. Does the activity advance the use of the product, or communicate science objectively?
  2. Initiator. Did the recipient request the information, or is the company pushing it proactively?
  3. Content. Is the information balanced, substantiated, and consistent with the evidence and the label?

The answers place the activity on one side of the boundary or send it to review. Fair balance means that benefit information is accompanied by a proportionate presentation of risk; substantiation means that each claim is supported by adequate evidence that a reviewer can trace. Both are conditions on content that apply regardless of channel.

The following table applies the three-part test to common activities. It is illustrative application of the principles above, not an exhaustive rule set; borderline activities go to review.

Activity Usual classification Determining factor
MSL discusses published pivotal-trial data in scientific exchange Nonpromotional Balanced, scientific, not advancing use
Sales representative distributes a benefit claim from the label Promotional Intent to advance use; governed by advertising rules
Medical information answers an unsolicited off-label question Nonpromotional Recipient initiated; balanced scientific response
Proactive email previewing an unapproved use Promotional and likely impermissible Company-initiated, off-label, advances use

3.2 The legal and ethical frameworks at a working level

A medical or evidence professional is expected to recognize the following frameworks and to know when an activity implicates one, then route it to compliance or legal. The BCMAEP standard is recognition and routing, not memorization of statutory text.

  • Anti-Kickback Statute (US). Prohibits knowingly offering, paying, soliciting, or receiving anything of value to induce or reward referrals or the purchase of items or services reimbursed by federal health-care programs. Its relevance to medical affairs is that payments and transfers of value to health-care professionals, including consulting fees, advisory-board honoraria, and research support, must reflect fair market value and a legitimate need, not an inducement to prescribe (HHS Office of Inspector General, Fraud and Abuse Laws).
  • False Claims Act (US). Imposes liability for causing false or fraudulent claims for payment to the government. In the industry it has been the basis for enforcement where off-label promotion or improper inducements were alleged to have caused improper claims (HHS Office of Inspector General, Fraud and Abuse Laws).
  • Physician Payments Sunshine Act (US). Requires manufacturers to report payments and other transfers of value to covered recipients, which are published in the public CMS Open Payments database. It is the transparency mechanism discussed in section 3.5 (CMS, Open Payments).
  • Off-label communication rules (US). Restrict promotional communication of uses outside the approved label, while preserving defined lawful channels for scientific and payer communication, including responses to unsolicited requests and pre-approval information exchange (sections 3.3 and 3.4) (FDA, Responding to Unsolicited Requests for Off-Label Information).
  • PhRMA Code on Interactions with Health Care Professionals. An industry self-regulatory code setting voluntary standards for interactions with health-care professionals, including limits on gifts, meals, and entertainment and expectations for the conduct of consulting and speaker arrangements (PhRMA, Code on Interactions with Health Care Professionals).
  • Good-practice (GxP) quality systems. The umbrella of regulated quality systems, including good clinical, laboratory, manufacturing, and pharmacovigilance practice, that govern how regulated activities are conducted and documented. GxP is a definitional umbrella term rather than a single statute; the individual practices are set out in the relevant FDA and ICH guidances.

The common thread is that legitimate scientific and business needs must be documented, valued fairly, and kept separate from any inducement to prescribe or purchase. A useful habit is to ask, of any interaction that involves a transfer of value, whether it would withstand publication in a newspaper and in the public transparency database.

3.3 Unsolicited requests and the scientific response

A key permissible channel is the response to an unsolicited request. When a health-care professional, on their own initiative, asks a specific scientific question, including a question about an unapproved use, the company may provide a truthful, balanced, nonpromotional, scientifically substantiated response, typically routed through medical information rather than through commercial staff (FDA, Responding to Unsolicited Requests for Off-Label Information About Prescription Drugs and Medical Devices).

The distinction from promotion rests on initiation and intent: the professional asked, the response is scientific and balanced, and it is not used as a device to promote. Medical information functions maintain standard response documents and a controlled intake process precisely so that these responses are consistent, reviewed, and documented (developed in Module 12). A career changer should note the failure modes: soliciting the question in order to answer it, steering an interaction toward an unapproved use, or attaching promotional material to a scientific response all convert a permissible response into promotion.

3.4 Pre-approval and post-approval information exchange with payers

Payers and health technology assessment (HTA) bodies must plan budgets and formularies before a product is approved, which creates a need for early, factual information exchange. Defined pathways permit certain communication of pre-approval clinical and economic information to population-health decision-makers, and separate expectations govern post-approval communication.

AMCP has published guidance on the responsible exchange of pre-approval and post-approval clinical and economic evidence with payers (AMCP, pre-approval and post-approval clinical and economic evidence guidance). The AMCP Format for Formulary Submissions provides the structured dossier through which such evidence is presented to a payer (AMCP, Format for Formulary Submissions guidance). The practical point for medical affairs is that pre-approval exchange is a defined and constrained activity, not an open license: the content must be factual and unbiased, the audience is limited to population-health decision-makers rather than individual prescribers, and the pre-approval and post-approval contexts carry different requirements. This is developed further in Module 9.

3.5 Transparency and aggregate spend reporting

Payments and transfers of value from manufacturers to covered health-care professionals and teaching hospitals are subject to public reporting under the Physician Payments Sunshine Act, and the reported data are published in the searchable federal CMS Open Payments database (CMS, Open Payments). The operational consequence for medical affairs is that consulting fees, advisory-board honoraria, meals, travel, and research support are captured, aggregated, and disclosed, so every such transfer must be defensible on its face.

A professional planning an advisory board or a consulting engagement (Module 13) should assume the payment will be public and should ensure it reflects fair market value for a genuine need, is supported by a written need assessment and a contract, and is not scaled to prescribing. The transparency regime does not prohibit these payments; it makes them visible, which changes how they must be justified.

3.6 Medical, legal, and regulatory review and the medical reviewer

Externally facing materials and many activities pass through a medical, legal, and regulatory (MLR) review before use. The reviewers represent distinct interests:

  • Legal assesses statutory and litigation risk.
  • Regulatory assesses label consistency and compliance with promotional rules.
  • Medical assesses scientific accuracy, balance, and substantiation.

Materials and activities that commonly require review include:

  • externally facing scientific and educational materials, slide decks, and enduring content;
  • standard response documents used by medical information;
  • congress abstracts, posters, and presentations;
  • payer-facing evidence dossiers and value materials; and
  • any material that makes a claim about the product or its evidence.

The medical reviewer's specific role is to confirm that every claim is scientifically accurate, supported by adequate and correctly cited evidence, presented with fair balance, and consistent with the data and the label. This is where the citation discipline taught throughout this program becomes an operational control: a claim a medical reviewer cannot trace to an adequate source does not pass review, and a reference that does not support the claim attached to it is a finding.

The requirement in the program AI-use policy that AI-assisted content receive human review before external release is an extension of the same MLR principle to a new class of tool (AI-use policy). A professional who internalizes the medical reviewer's standard will produce work that passes review the first time.

3.7 Privacy and confidentiality

Medical and evidence activities routinely touch personal and confidential information, and two regimes are most often implicated at a working level. In the US, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) governs the use and disclosure of individually identifiable health information held by covered entities and their business associates (HHS, Health Information Privacy (HIPAA)). In the European Union, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) governs the processing of personal data, treats health data as a special category with heightened protection, and constrains cross-border transfer (Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), EUR-Lex).

The working rules for a medical professional are consistent across both regimes:

  • collect only the data needed for a defined purpose;
  • do not disclose identifiable data without a lawful basis;
  • de-identify or aggregate wherever possible;
  • store and transfer data only through approved, secured systems; and
  • never enter identifiable or confidential data into a tool or environment that is not approved for it.

In real-world evidence and outcomes work (Module 7), where individual-level records are analyzed, these rules take a specific form: data are used under an appropriate legal and governance basis, de-identified to the extent the analysis allows, and held in an approved environment rather than a general-purpose tool. The default when a data-handling question is unresolved is to exclude the data, not to proceed and seek forgiveness.

That last rule is the privacy foundation of the AI-use policy (AI-use policy), which defaults to exclusion of patient-level and confidential company data from AI tools that are not validated, approved, and contractually governed.

3.8 Ethics beyond the law: conflicts of interest and integrity

Compliance defines the legal floor; professional ethics sets a higher expectation that a medical professional carries into industry. Two obligations recur. The first is the disclosure and management of conflicts of interest. In publications, authors are required to disclose relationships that could be perceived to influence the work, and the responsibility for accuracy and integrity rests with the human authors (ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors); Good Publication Practice 2022 embeds the same expectation of transparency in company-sponsored research (DeTora et al., 2022, GPP 2022). The second obligation is scientific integrity: presenting evidence completely, including results that do not favor the product, and not allowing selection, emphasis, or omission to mislead. A truthful statement can still be misleading through selective presentation, which is why fair balance and substantiation are ethical as well as regulatory requirements. The working principle is that a medical professional's credibility, and therefore usefulness, depends on being trusted to state the evidence as it is.

3.9 Common failure modes and how to route them

The classification skill is best learned through the errors it prevents. Five failure modes recur, each with a routing response.

  • Manufacturing an unsolicited request. Prompting a professional to ask a question so the company can answer it converts a scientific response into promotion. Route: confirm the request was genuinely unsolicited before responding, and document it.
  • Assuming truthful equals permissible. A statement can be accurate and still promotional or misleading through channel, initiator, or selective emphasis. Route: apply the three-part test and, when in doubt, send to review.
  • Forgetting that transfers of value are public. An honorarium set without a documented need or fair-market-value basis becomes a transparency and anti-kickback exposure once published. Route: document the need and the valuation before the engagement.
  • Entering confidential or patient data into an unapproved tool. This breaches privacy and confidentiality regardless of intent. Route: use only validated, approved environments; default to exclusion.
  • Selecting a reprint or dataset to imply an off-label benefit. Distribution and context can make truthful data promotional. Route: send the plan to medical, legal, and regulatory review.

In every case the safe action is the same: classify the activity, route ambiguous cases to review, and document the reasoning. That reasoning is what the capstone compliance memo captures.

3.10 Documentation and the audit trail

A recurring principle across every framework in this module is that compliance is demonstrated through documentation. An interaction that was compliant but undocumented is difficult to defend, and a transfer of value without a written need assessment invites the inference of inducement. The working habit is to record, contemporaneously:

  • the initiator and nature of a scientific request and the response provided;
  • the need assessment, fair-market-value basis, and contract for any engagement involving payment;
  • the review decisions made in medical, legal, and regulatory review; and
  • the data handling and any use of an AI tool, per the audit log defined in the program AI-use policy (AI-use policy).

The same discipline that a medical reviewer applies to a claim, that it be traceable to an adequate source, applies to an activity: it should be traceable to a documented, defensible rationale. This is why the applied activity below is a written memo rather than a verbal judgment.

Worked example

Classifying pre-approval and post-approval payer communication

Case E in the case library uses the AMCP Format and its pre- and post-approval information-exchange guidance (AMCP, Format for Formulary Submissions guidance; AMCP, pre-approval and post-approval clinical and economic evidence guidance).

Take three proposed communications to a payer for a product still under review:

  • (a) an unsolicited request from the payer's pharmacy director for the pivotal-trial results and a budget-impact estimate;
  • (b) a proactive email from a sales representative previewing the expected label and price; and
  • (c) a medical-affairs presentation of the pre-approval evidence dossier at the payer's request.

Classify each. Item (a) is a permissible response to an unsolicited request, routed through medical or a designated function, provided the content is factual and balanced. Item (b) is impermissible: it is proactive, commercial, and promotes an unapproved product. Item (c) can fall within pre-approval information exchange if it follows the AMCP guidance, uses factual and unbiased content, is directed to a population-health decision-maker, and is delivered by the appropriate function. The exercise is to state the intent, the initiator, and the content test for each, and to identify the single change that would move an item across the boundary (for example, if the company had initiated item (a), it would no longer be an unsolicited request).

Applied activity

Produces capstone work product 2

For the assigned capstone product scenario (case J in the case library), you will receive a set of five to eight proposed activities spanning field medical, publications, payer engagement, and advisory work. Write a compliance decision memo that classifies each activity as promotional, nonpromotional, or requiring review, and for each states the rationale using the classification aid below.

Question Why it matters
What is the intent of the activity? Advancing use signals promotion
Who initiated it? An unsolicited request opens a scientific-response channel
Is the content balanced and substantiated? Unbalanced or unsupported content fails regardless of channel
Which function should own it? Places the activity relative to the commercial firewall
Which framework is implicated? Transparency, pre-approval exchange, or anti-kickback routing
Does it touch personal or confidential data? Triggers the privacy control

This memo is capstone work product 2, and its classifications constrain every downstream deliverable in the portfolio.

AI-use focus

AI-use focus

This module establishes the compliance constraints that the program AI-use policy then codifies. Three constraints follow directly from the frameworks above.

First, confidential and patient-level data are not entered into AI tools that are not validated, approved, and contractually governed, which is the privacy principle of section 3.7 applied to AI. Second, AI-assisted content is disclosed where it is shared, consistent with publication-ethics expectations: the ICMJE recommendations require disclosure of AI-assisted technologies and hold the human authors, not the tool, responsible for accuracy and integrity, because a chatbot cannot be an author (ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors), and Good Publication Practice 2022 addresses the compliant incorporation of such tools (DeTora et al., 2022, GPP 2022). Third, no AI output is presented as fact without verification, and no citation, DOI, or data point is fabricated; a gap is marked instead. These constraints are the operative standard in AI-use policy and are taught in full in Module 14.

Intent, whether it advances use; initiator, whether the recipient requested it; and content, whether it is balanced and adequately substantiated.

2Why can the same accurate scientific statement be permissible in one context and impermissible in another?Show answer
Model answer

Because compliance governs intent, initiator, and channel, not only truth; a balanced response to an unsolicited request differs from a proactive promotional message with identical content.

It follows pre-approval information-exchange expectations, is factual, unbiased, and balanced, is directed to a population-health decision-maker, and is delivered by the appropriate function, consistent with the AMCP guidance.

Model answer

Legal, regulatory, and medical; the medical reviewer confirms scientific accuracy, adequate and correctly cited substantiation, fair balance, and label consistency.

7Why can a chatbot not be listed as an author on a manuscript?Show answer

It cannot take responsibility for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the work; the ICMJE places that responsibility on the human authors.

8Distinguish fair balance from substantiation.Show answer

Fair balance requires that benefit claims be accompanied by a proportionate presentation of risk; substantiation requires that each claim be supported by adequate, traceable evidence.

9Why is documentation treated as part of compliance rather than administrative overhead?Show answer

Examples: distributing a reprint selected to imply an off-label benefit; presenting benefit results while omitting proportionate risk information; company-initiated delivery of accurate but off-label claims.
