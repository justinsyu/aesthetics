The pharmaceutical, biotechnology, and medtech industry and the drug development lifecycle

Domain A (Industry orientation and translation). Approximately 5 hours.

Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

  1. Map the phases of drug discovery and development from target identification through post-marketing, and place regulatory and evidence milestones on that map. (A, B)
  2. Distinguish the development pathways for small molecules, biologics, cell and gene therapies, devices, diagnostics, and combination products. (B)
  3. Describe the roles of sponsors, contract research organizations, regulators, payers, health systems, and patients across the lifecycle. (A)
  4. Explain how public research funding and private development connect in the therapeutic ecosystem. (A)

Why this matters for the career changer

A clinician evaluates a therapy at the point of care: is it approved, is it indicated, does the patient benefit. Industry work sits upstream and alongside that decision. It asks how the evidence that supports the label was generated, what evidence payers and health technology assessment (HTA) bodies still need, and how a medical team communicates that evidence without promoting. To contribute, a new industry professional needs a working model of the whole lifecycle and of where each function attaches. This module builds that model; every later module attaches to it.

1.1 The industry and its segments

The industry that develops medicines and medical technologies spans several segments: pharmaceutical companies (traditionally small-molecule drugs), biotechnology companies (biologic and advanced-therapy products), and medical-technology companies (devices and diagnostics). Many organizations now span more than one segment, and the field as a whole covers pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, medical devices, diagnostics, and emerging areas such as artificial intelligence.

The core economic feature of the industry is that development is long, expensive, and high-risk: most candidate molecules that enter development do not reach approval, and the cost of the successes must cover the cost of the failures. This attrition is why evidence planning, prioritization, and value demonstration (the subjects of Domain C) are central rather than peripheral activities.

The public sector is part of this system. Basic research funded by the National Institutes of Health and other public bodies produces much of the early science that private development builds on, and the handoffs between public discovery, private development, pricing, and access are the subject of ongoing policy analysis (National Academies, NIH's role in drug development, workshop proceedings).

1.2 The development lifecycle

A conventional way to describe drug development is a sequence of overlapping stages. The learner should be able to place evidence and regulatory milestones on this sequence.

  • Discovery and target identification. Identifying a biological target and candidate molecules; early screening.
  • Preclinical development. Laboratory and animal studies of pharmacology and toxicology to support first-in-human testing.
  • Phase I. First-in-human studies, typically in a small number of healthy volunteers or patients, focused on safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics.
  • Phase II. Studies in patients to assess preliminary efficacy, dose, and safety.
  • Phase III. Larger, typically randomized and controlled studies to confirm efficacy and characterize safety in the intended population; these usually support the marketing application.
  • Regulatory review and approval. Submission of a marketing application and agency review (see Module 5).
  • Phase IV and post-marketing. Studies and surveillance after approval, including safety monitoring, additional evidence generation, and evidence for payers and HTA bodies (see Modules 6 to 10).

The distinction between an interventional clinical trial and other study types matters from the first week, because it drives protocol writing, registration, and regulatory documentation. The NIH clinical-trial definition case studies are a useful drill: for each scenario, decide whether the study meets the clinical-trial definition before revealing the answer (NIH, Clinical Trial Definition Case Studies).

Modeling and simulation increasingly inform decisions across this sequence. Model-informed drug development uses quantitative models to support dose selection, trial design, and evidence synthesis, and to make regulatory interactions more efficient (FDA, Application of Model-Informed Drug Development in Study Design and Analysis).

1.3 Modality-specific pathways

The lifecycle above is a common frame, but the modality changes the evidence and regulatory specifics.

  • Small molecules are chemically synthesized drugs; development follows the classical new-drug pathway.
  • Biologics are large, complex molecules produced in living systems; they follow a biologics pathway and raise manufacturing and immunogenicity considerations.
  • Cell and gene therapies are advanced therapies with distinctive durability, safety, and manufacturing questions, often studied in small rare-disease populations.
  • Devices are regulated by risk class, with distinct premarket pathways.
  • Diagnostics support treatment decisions and are central to precision-medicine strategies; companion diagnostics may be co-developed with a therapy.
  • Combination products join two or more of the above and require coordinated regulatory handling.

Device classification, combination products, and diagnostic development are distinct areas of knowledge alongside drug development. For a non-specialist audience, the EUPATI open educational materials give a useful overview of how medicines move from discovery to market and where patients are involved (EUPATI, discovery and development of medicines).

1.4 Stakeholders and where value is decided

A useful discipline is to name, for any product, the stakeholders and what each needs:

  • Sponsors (the developing company) decide which evidence to generate and bear the development risk.
  • Contract research organizations and other vendors execute studies and services.
  • Regulators (for example the FDA and EMA) decide whether the evidence supports approval and what the label says (Module 5).
  • HTA bodies and payers decide whether and how the product is covered, priced, and reimbursed, using clinical and economic evidence (Modules 8 and 9).
  • Clinicians decide whether to use the product for a given patient.
  • Patients and caregivers experience the outcomes and increasingly contribute to evidence and to R&D decisions (EUPATI, guidance for patient involvement in industry-led medicines R&D).

The central insight for a new industry professional is that regulatory approval is necessary but not sufficient: value is decided again by payers and HTA bodies, and often again by clinicians and patients. Evidence must be planned for all of these decisions, not only for approval. This is the rationale for integrated evidence generation planning (Module 10).

1.5 Where medical affairs and evidence functions attach

Medical affairs and evidence functions attach across the whole lifecycle, not only after launch:

  • In development, they inform evidence strategy, endpoint relevance, and scientific engagement with external experts.
  • Around approval, they build scientific communication platforms, publication plans, and field-medical readiness.
  • After launch, they generate real-world and outcomes evidence, support appropriate use, respond to medical inquiries, and feed insights back into strategy.

Module 2 develops this operating model in detail.

Worked example

Placing a product on the lifecycle

Take odevixibat, one of the FDA LEADER 3D teaching cases, developed for a rare cholestatic liver disease (FDA LEADER 3D odevixibat case). Working from the public case, a learner can place the milestones: a rare-disease population that constrains trial size, an endpoint chosen to capture clinically meaningful change, a regulatory pathway suited to small populations, and post-approval evidence needs for payers. FDA states these cases are teaching scenarios, not approval templates, and that rare-disease development is assessed case by case (FDA, LEADER 3D program); the exercise is to reason about the map, not to copy a strategy.

Applied activity

Produces capstone work product 1

Using the assigned capstone product scenario (case J in the case library), draft a one-page lifecycle map that marks: the development phase the product is in; the regulatory pathway milestones ahead; the points at which HTA and payer evidence will be needed; and where each medical affairs function attaches. This map becomes the first component of the capstone product situation brief.

Knowledge check

Answer each question from memory before revealing the model answer. Retrieval practice of this kind improves retention relative to re-reading (Adesope et al., 2017).

1A study prospectively assigns participants to an intervention to evaluate its effect on a health outcome. Is it a clinical trial, and why does the answer matter?Show answer
Model answer

Yes, it meets the interventional clinical-trial definition, which triggers registration, protocol, and regulatory documentation obligations; see the NIH case studies.

2Why is regulatory approval necessary but not sufficient for patient access?Show answer
Model answer

Payers and HTA bodies make separate coverage, pricing, and reimbursement decisions using clinical and economic evidence.

3Name three stakeholders whose evidence needs differ, and give one evidence need for each.Show answer
Model answer

Examples: regulators need pivotal efficacy and safety; payers need comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness; clinicians need applicability to their patients.

4What does model-informed drug development contribute to the lifecycle?Show answer
Model answer

Quantitative models to support dose selection, trial design, evidence synthesis, and regulatory interaction.

5Give one reason attrition in development makes evidence prioritization important.Show answer
Model answer

Because resources are finite and most candidates fail, teams must prioritize the evidence that most changes decisions.
