Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

  1. Define the functions of medical affairs (field medical, medical information, publications, medical communications, health economics and outcomes research (HEOR), medical operations, and medical strategy) and describe the output of each. (A)
  2. Explain the interfaces between medical affairs and clinical development, regulatory, safety, commercial, and market access, and the handoffs between them. (A)
  3. Describe the medical affairs strategic planning cycle and the content and inputs of the medical plan. (A, G)
  4. Articulate the value and impact measures used for medical affairs functions and state their limitations. (A, G)

Why this matters for the career changer

A clinician who has worked with industry has usually met one function of medical affairs: the medical science liaison (MSL) who visited to discuss data. That is a small part of the operating model. Inside a company, evidence is generated, reviewed, published, communicated, and defended by a set of distinct functions with different remits, and a new professional is expected to know which function owns which task, who hands off to whom, and where the boundary with commercial promotion sits.

Getting this wrong has consequences in both directions. A professional who does not know that a publication has a plan and an authorship standard, or that a medical-information response follows a controlled process, will duplicate work or breach a control. A professional who does not know where the commercial firewall runs will create a compliance problem. Module 1 placed medical affairs on the lifecycle; this module opens the function and shows its internal structure and its external interfaces. Module 3 then adds the compliance boundary that constrains every activity described here.

2.1 What medical affairs is, and the competency view

Medical affairs is the scientific and evidence function of a pharmaceutical, biotechnology, or medical-technology company. It is distinct from commercial, which promotes, and from clinical development, which runs the registrational trials; its work is nonpromotional scientific exchange and evidence generation. Its purpose is to ensure that the right evidence is generated and that it reaches scientific, payer, and clinical audiences accurately and without promotion.

A practical way to define the function is by the competencies it requires. Across the field, medical affairs work is commonly organized into a set of competency domains that span scientific and clinical knowledge, evidence generation, customer engagement and scientific communication, business and strategy, leadership, and medical governance and compliance. This program uses a seven-domain competency framework of this kind (handbook, section 5), and each function below maps to one or more of those domains.

The competency view matters for a career changer because it makes explicit that medical affairs is not a single job. It is a set of specialized roles, each with its own body of knowledge, and the BCMAEP curriculum is built to develop several of them. Recognizing which competency a task belongs to is the first step to knowing which function owns it.

It also helps to fix the boundaries against the two adjacent scientific-facing functions. Clinical development designs and runs the registrational trials that support the marketing application; it hands the resulting evidence to medical affairs, which generates further evidence, communicates it, and gathers scientific insight after and around approval. Commercial promotes the approved product within the label. Medical affairs sits between these, generating and communicating evidence without promoting, and the distinction is not organizational tidiness: it is the basis of the compliance boundary developed in Module 3.

2.2 The functions of medical affairs

The program uses the following taxonomy of functions. Each has a defined output and is developed in a later module.

  • Field medical (medical science liaisons). Field-based scientists who conduct nonpromotional scientific exchange with external experts, support investigator interactions, and capture medical insights that inform strategy. Deployment models, territory design, and the ratio of MSLs to external experts are operational decisions with cost and coverage consequences (IQVIA, Medical Science Liaisons white paper). The role is a well-defined field-medical specialty, developed in Module 11.
  • Medical information. Responds to unsolicited requests for medical and scientific information from clinicians, patients, and payers, using standard response documents and a compliant intake and review process. Developed in Module 12.
  • Publications. Plans and executes the peer-reviewed communication of company-sponsored research under Good Publication Practice 2022, which sets ethics, transparency, authorship, and disclosure standards (DeTora et al., 2022, GPP 2022). Developed in Module 12.
  • Medical communications. Builds the scientific communication platform, congress activity, and enduring educational materials that carry the evidence narrative to scientific audiences. Developed in Module 12.
  • HEOR. Generates and communicates economic and outcomes evidence for payers and health technology assessment (HTA) bodies. Developed in Modules 8 and 9.
  • Medical operations. Provides the systems, training, budgeting, and process governance that let the other functions run compliantly, including the medical, legal, and regulatory review workflow described in Module 3.
  • Medical strategy. Sets the objectives and the medical plan that align all of the above to the product and to the evidence needs across the lifecycle.

The following table summarizes the taxonomy and where each function is developed in the program.

Function Primary output Principal audience Developed in
Field medical (MSL) Scientific exchange and routed insights External experts, investigators Module 11
Medical information Standard response documents Clinicians, patients, payers Module 12
Publications Peer-reviewed manuscripts and abstracts Scientific community Module 12
Medical communications Scientific platform, congress content Scientific and internal audiences Module 12
HEOR Economic and outcomes evidence Payers, HTA bodies Modules 8, 9

2.3 Organizational structures: global, regional, and affiliate

Medical affairs is usually organized across three levels. A global function sets therapeutic-area strategy, the core scientific platform, and the evidence priorities. Regional functions adapt that strategy to a group of markets. Country or affiliate functions execute locally, engage local experts, and meet local regulatory and payer requirements.

Level Primary ownership Example decision
Global Therapeutic-area strategy, core scientific platform, evidence priorities Which pivotal-trial narrative leads the platform
Regional Adaptation of strategy to a market group Regional congress and expert-engagement approach
Affiliate or country Local execution, local experts, local compliance A local advisory board conducted under the local code

The same activity, for example an advisory board or a publication, may be planned globally but must be delivered under local rules, and the compliance boundary (Module 3) is drawn locally as well as globally. A career changer joining at affiliate level should understand two things: the strategy they execute was often set globally, and the insights they capture locally are intended to travel back up to inform it. MAPS launch and strategy materials treat this global-to-local alignment as a core operating challenge, particularly for smaller organizations with limited resources (MAPS, launch-excellence masterclass brochure).

2.4 The medical plan and the strategic planning cycle

The medical plan is the central planning artifact of the function. It translates the product strategy and the external scientific environment into a set of medical objectives, and it maps those objectives to activities across field medical, publications, communications, evidence generation, and medical information.

Its inputs include:

  • the product's evidence base and the gaps in it;
  • the competitive and scientific landscape;
  • external-expert, investigator, and payer insights;
  • the regulatory and access milestones ahead; and
  • the resources and budget available.

The planning cycle is iterative and typically moves through four stages:

  1. Set. Translate strategy and the external environment into medical objectives and prioritized activities.
  2. Execute. Deliver the activities across the functions, on the medical side of the commercial firewall.
  3. Measure. Assess progress against objectives using the value and impact measures discussed in section 2.7.
  4. Revise. Update objectives and activities as insights and evidence accumulate, then repeat.

The plan is therefore a living document rather than an annual artifact filed and forgotten. MAPS workshop materials frame this as converting recurring operational challenges, such as constrained budgets and unclear priorities, into a structured plan with defined objectives (MAPS, Challenges to Opportunities workshop). Because the medical plan draws on evidence gaps and stakeholder needs, it is closely related to the integrated evidence generation plan built in Module 10; the medical plan is the annual operating expression, and the integrated evidence plan is the cross-functional evidence expression, of the same underlying strategy.

2.5 Interfaces and the commercial firewall

Medical affairs does not operate alone. Its value depends on well-defined handoffs with adjacent functions.

Interface What medical affairs contributes Handoff or boundary
Clinical development Endpoint relevance, post-approval evidence needs Takes over evidence generation after pivotal trials
Regulatory Scientific and label-related support Ensures external communication is label-consistent
Safety and pharmacovigilance Capture and routing of adverse events from the field Reporting duty and timelines (Module 6)
Commercial Nonpromotional scientific context Firewall: medical does not promote or direct promotion
Market access and HEOR Clinical and economic evidence for payers Separate access decision from regulatory approval

The commercial interface carries the sharpest constraint. Medical affairs conducts nonpromotional scientific exchange; commercial conducts promotion. A firewall separates the two so that nonpromotional activity is neither directed by, nor used as, promotion. Module 3 defines this boundary in detail; for now the operating point is that the firewall determines which function may own a given task.

Interfaces fail in predictable ways: an evidence need identified late in development that could have been designed into the pivotal trial; a publication that lands after the payer decision it was meant to inform; a field insight that is captured but never routed to strategy. Each is a handoff failure rather than a failure of any single function, which is why the responsibility map in the applied activity makes ownership and timing explicit.

A recurring theme across these interfaces is that evidence must be planned jointly rather than in silos. The case for integrating evidence generation across development, medical affairs, HEOR, and access, so that evidence flows from bench to budget, is made explicitly in the current literature (Schneeweiss and Miksad, 2025). One tool for aligning development with downstream payer and HTA needs is a dynamic target product profile that carries payer perspectives into development decisions (Wang et al., 2022). A related body of work examines how to obtain the right evidence after approval, when regulatory questions give way to access and effectiveness questions (Vreman et al., 2020).

2.6 The evolving strategic role of medical affairs

Historically, medical affairs was described as a support function that executed activities defined elsewhere. Industry and society materials now describe a shift toward medical affairs as a strategic contributor that shapes evidence strategy and represents the scientific and patient perspective in enterprise decisions, particularly around launch (MAPS, launch-excellence masterclass brochure; MAPS/Amedea, innovation and metrics webinar). The integrated-evidence argument reinforces this position: if evidence must be planned across the whole lifecycle from bench to budget, a function that spans development, publication, field engagement, and outcomes evidence is well placed to coordinate it (Schneeweiss and Miksad, 2025). These are advocacy and educational sources rather than empirical studies, and a professional should read them as descriptions of an intended direction, not as measured outcomes. The practical implication is that the strategic contribution of medical affairs is judged by the quality of its evidence and insight, which is why measuring that contribution is both important and difficult.

2.7 Value and impact measures and their limitations

Because medical affairs is nonpromotional, it cannot be measured by sales, and measuring its value is genuinely difficult. Two categories of measure are used.

Type Example Strength Limitation
Activity Scientific exchanges per MSL per quarter Easy to collect, comparable Rewards volume, not value
Activity Publications submitted Objective, countable Insensitive to impact or reach
Outcome Insights that changed a plan decision Tied to strategy Hard to attribute
Outcome Evidence gaps closed Meaningful Long lag, multiple contributors

Activity measures count what was done; outcome measures attempt to capture effect. Activity measures are easy to collect but weakly connected to value; outcome measures are more meaningful but harder to attribute. MAPS and industry commentators have argued for shifting the balance toward outcome-oriented metrics while acknowledging the attribution problem (MAPS/Amedea, innovation and metrics webinar). The limitation a new professional should internalize is that a metric that is easy to count is not necessarily a metric that reflects value, and that a plan measured only by activity can be busy without being effective.

2.8 Medical insights and the feedback loop

Insights are the mechanism by which the operating model learns. A medical insight is a structured, actionable observation gathered from an external expert or the scientific environment, for example an unmet evidence need, a question about how a therapy performs in a subgroup, or a barrier to appropriate use. Field medical is the primary channel: MSLs gather insights during scientific exchange, and deployment models are designed in part around this capture function alongside scientific engagement (IQVIA, Medical Science Liaisons white paper).

Captured insights are structured, routed, and synthesized so that they inform the medical plan and the evidence strategy. The value of the field function depends as much on the quality of the insights it returns as on the number of exchanges it conducts, which is why the metric redesign in section 2.7 targets routed insights rather than raw contact counts. Vendor intelligence platforms are increasingly used to map external experts and aggregate insight signals, as in the autoimmune expert-intelligence case in the case library (MAPS/H1, autoimmune KOL intelligence case study); a professional should separate evidence-based insight from vendor positioning. Insight capture and synthesis are developed in Module 11; the structural point here is that without a functioning feedback loop the operating model executes activities but does not learn from them.

2.9 How the functions collaborate: a launch vignette

A product launch shows the operating model in motion and previews the applied activity below. In the run-up to launch, medical strategy sets the objectives; publications sequences the primary and secondary manuscripts under Good Publication Practice (DeTora et al., 2022, GPP 2022); medical communications assembles the scientific platform and congress plan; field medical prepares the scientific narrative and engagement plan and stands ready to capture insights; medical information finalizes the standard response documents for anticipated questions; and HEOR completes the payer evidence package. Each of these is nonpromotional and sits on the medical side of the firewall, while commercial prepares promotional materials separately and subject to review. MAPS launch materials treat this coordination, especially under resource constraints, as a defining test of the operating model (MAPS, launch-excellence masterclass brochure). The applied activity asks you to make these ownerships and handoffs explicit for the capstone product.

2.10 Medical operations and governance

Medical operations is the least visible function but the one that most determines whether the others can work. Its remit typically includes:

  • process and standard-operating-procedure governance, including the medical, legal, and regulatory review workflow (Module 3);
  • training and onboarding, so that field and office staff apply the compliance boundary consistently;
  • planning, budgeting, and resource allocation across the medical plan;
  • data, systems, and technology, including customer-relationship and insight-management platforms and, increasingly, AI tools; and
  • the metrics and reporting infrastructure that supplies the measures discussed in section 2.7.

The adoption of digital, analytics, and AI capabilities is now a defined operations agenda, and a 2024 benchmark of 32 companies documents where the function currently stands on that adoption (MAPS, Digital, Advanced Analytics and AI in Medical Affairs benchmark report, 2024). For a career changer, a well-run operations function is why a compliant medical-information response reaches a clinician quickly and why an audit finds a clean documentation trail.

2.11 Common misconceptions for the career changer

Several assumptions carried from clinical practice cause early errors, and correcting them is part of the transition.

  • Medical affairs is not sales. The MSL is a scientific role bound by the nonpromotional standard; treating it as a commercial role misunderstands the firewall (Module 3) and creates compliance risk.
  • Medical affairs is broader than field medical. The visible MSL is one of seven functions in section 2.2; publications, medical information, HEOR, communications, operations, and strategy are equally part of the model.
  • Publications are planned, not ad hoc. A manuscript follows a publication plan and Good Publication Practice, with defined authorship and disclosure standards, rather than being written and submitted opportunistically (DeTora et al., 2022, GPP 2022).
  • Activity is not value. As section 2.7 shows, counting interactions or outputs measures effort, not effect; a busy plan is not necessarily an effective one.
  • Approval is not the finish line. As established in Module 1, payers and HTA bodies make separate decisions, and much of the medical affairs and evidence work begins at launch.
Worked example

Critiquing and redesigning a medical affairs metric

Case I in the case library assembles public MAPS materials on medical affairs strategy and metrics, including the "Converting Challenges Into Opportunities" workshop and an innovation-and-metrics webinar (MAPS, Challenges to Opportunities workshop; MAPS/Amedea, innovation and metrics webinar).

Working from these, take a common field-medical key performance indicator, the number of scientific engagements per MSL per quarter, and critique it. It is an activity measure: it rewards volume, is insensitive to the quality or influence of each interaction, and can be met without advancing any medical objective. Redesign it toward an outcome logic, for example the proportion of engagements that produced a documented, routed insight that was later cited in a plan revision, while noting the attribution and data-quality limits of the redesigned measure: an insight may take quarters to influence a decision, and several functions may share credit. The exercise is to reason about what the metric actually incentivizes, not to adopt any vendor's dashboard as a validated standard.

Applied activity

Contributes to capstone work product 1

Using the assigned capstone product scenario (case J in the case library) at a defined launch stage, build a cross-functional responsibility map. Begin with the main evidence and communication tasks for the launch, for example:

  • publication of the pivotal trial;
  • the scientific communication platform;
  • field-medical readiness and engagement planning;
  • the medical-information standard-response set;
  • the payer evidence package; and
  • adverse-event capture and safety reporting.

For each task, identify the owning function, the contributing functions, and the handoff to or firewall with commercial, and mark where an activity is nonpromotional and must stay on the medical side of the firewall. This responsibility map is added to the capstone product situation brief (work product 1), extending the lifecycle map produced in Module 1.

AI-use focus

AI-use focus

Permitted: use an AI assistant to draft a first-pass stakeholder-and-responsibility map and a candidate medical affairs metrics framework for the assigned scenario.

Required controls: a human reviews the draft for accuracy and for compliance, in particular that no proposed activity blurs the nonpromotional boundary; verify any external claim about a function's remit against a primary source; do not enter confidential product information into a tool that is not validated and approved; and record the use in your audit log (tool, task, what you verified, who is accountable). Medical affairs organizations are adopting these tools rapidly, and a 2024 benchmark surveying 32 companies documents the current state of digital, advanced analytics, and AI adoption in the function (MAPS, Digital, Advanced Analytics and AI in Medical Affairs benchmark report, 2024). This activity applies the program AI-use policy (AI-use policy).
