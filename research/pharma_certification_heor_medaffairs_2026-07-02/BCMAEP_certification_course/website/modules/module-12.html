Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

  1. Build a scientific communication platform and publication plan. (D)
  2. Apply Good Publication Practice (GPP 2022) and authorship and disclosure standards, including standards for AI use in manuscripts. (D, F)
  3. Plan congress activity and nonpromotional scientific presentations, including HEOR impact-case abstracts. (D)
  4. Design and deliver compliant medical information responses. (D, F)

Why this matters for the career changer

Clinicians consume the scientific literature; they read the trial, the guideline, and the review. Industry scientific communication produces that literature and the surrounding scientific record, under rules that most clinicians never see.

Those rules exist because the author of a company-sponsored publication has a financial interest in the result. The response of the publication system has been to codify who may be an author, what support must be disclosed, and what may and may not be claimed. A career changer who treats a company publication like an academic paper written on personal time will violate these rules without intending to.

This module builds the specific competencies:

  • a scientific communication platform that keeps every channel consistent and accurate;
  • a publication plan that meets Good Publication Practice;
  • authorship, contributorship, and disclosure handled to ICMJE standards, including the treatment of AI;
  • nonpromotional congress abstracts, including the HEOR impact-case format;
  • compliant medical information responses.

It produces the nonpromotional congress abstract that completes capstone work product 7 and a standard medical-information response document.

12.1 The scientific communication platform and lexicon

A scientific communication platform is the internal reference document that states, in neutral scientific language, the current evidence-based narrative for a product or disease area:

  • the unmet need;
  • the mechanism and the key data;
  • the acknowledged limitations of the evidence;
  • the agreed scientific terminology (the lexicon).

Its purpose is consistency and accuracy: field medical (Module 11), publications, congresses, and medical information should all draw on the same evidence and describe it the same way. The platform is a scientific document, not a promotional one; it records what the evidence shows and does not show, and it is updated as evidence changes.

The lexicon is governed centrally so that the same term carries the same meaning across functions. Divergent terminology between the field, publications, and medical information is a common source of inconsistency and of inadvertent statements that drift beyond the evidence.

The competency of building and maintaining these communications is well established in the field, and it centers on three duties: developing a publication plan, implementing it, and fostering ethical and compliant publication practice.

Scientific communication sits within the program's scientific communication and field medical domain (Domain D), and building a coherent platform under data and budget constraints is a recognized medical affairs skill (MAPS, Converting Challenges Into Opportunities).

12.2 Publication planning and GPP 2022

A publication plan sequences the peer-reviewed communication of a product's evidence: which analyses become manuscripts or congress presentations, in what order, in what venues, and on what timeline.

The plan typically coordinates several publication assets, each with a distinct audience and function:

  • primary manuscripts reporting pivotal trial results;
  • secondary and subgroup analyses;
  • congress abstracts, posters, and oral presentations (see 12.5);
  • reviews, methods papers, and HEOR reports;
  • plain-language summaries that make results accessible to non-specialist readers.

Sequencing matters for the integrity of the record: the primary result should generally be published before or alongside derivative communications, and the same data should not be published redundantly across venues.

The governing standard for company-sponsored research is Good Publication Practice, updated as GPP 2022 (DeTora et al., 2022). GPP 2022 sets expectations that a career changer must internalize before drafting anything:

  • Transparency of the sponsor's role. The company's involvement in the research and in the publication is disclosed, not concealed.
  • Complete and non-selective reporting. Results are published so that the record is not distorted by selective or redundant publication.
  • Alignment of authorship with recognized criteria and disclosure of all professional medical writing support (see 12.3).
  • Steering and documentation. Publication decisions follow a documented, principled process rather than ad hoc commercial preference.

GPP 2022 is guidance on the ethics and transparency of publication; it complements, and does not replace, the authorship rules of the ICMJE (DeTora et al., 2022). A publication plan built to GPP is a compliance artifact as much as a scientific one, because failures of transparency in company-sponsored publication carry legal and reputational consequences (Module 3).

12.3 Authorship, contributorship, disclosure, and AI provisions

The ICMJE defines authorship by four criteria that must all be met:

  1. substantial contribution to conception or design, or to acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data;
  2. drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content;
  3. final approval of the version to be published;
  4. agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work (ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors).

Two consequences follow directly and are frequent failure points in industry practice:

  • Ghost and guest authorship are prohibited. A person who meets the criteria must be offered authorship; a person who does not meet them must not be listed. Professional medical writers who do not meet all four criteria are acknowledged as contributors with their role and funding disclosed, not hidden.
  • Contributorship and disclosure are explicit. Non-author contributions are recorded, and all authors disclose financial and other conflicts of interest (ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors).

On artificial intelligence, the ICMJE position is specific: AI tools and large language models cannot be listed as authors, because they cannot take responsibility for the work or be accountable for it, and authors who use AI in producing a manuscript must disclose that use and remain fully responsible for the accuracy and integrity of the content (ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors).

GPP 2022 similarly situates AI-assisted writing within the existing transparency and accountability framework rather than exempting it (DeTora et al., 2022). The practical rule for the learner: AI may assist drafting and literature triage, but the human authors are accountable, every AI-produced claim and citation is verified, and the AI use is disclosed as the journal requires and recorded in the audit log (Module 14).

12.4 Predatory-journal avoidance

A publication plan must select legitimate, peer-reviewed venues. Predatory journals solicit submissions and charge fees while providing little or no genuine peer review, and publishing company-sponsored evidence in such a venue both damages the scientific record and undermines the transparency that GPP 2022 requires (DeTora et al., 2022).

Practical screening questions before a venue is chosen:

  • Is the journal indexed in a recognized bibliographic database?
  • Is its peer-review process described and verifiable?
  • Are its editorial board and contact details genuine?
  • Are its fees and processes transparent and disclosed in advance?

When these cannot be confirmed, the venue is not used.

12.5 Congress planning and nonpromotional HEOR impact-case abstracts

Congress activity communicates evidence to the scientific and payer community through abstracts, posters, and presentations. Two constraints govern this work:

  • congress and medical-education activity conducted by the medical function is nonpromotional and follows the scientific communication platform;
  • HEOR and value evidence is presented to a defined nonpromotional standard set by the host society.

The ISPOR examples are the working models for this module. The HEOR impact-case abstract example shows the required structure and the nonpromotional discipline: generic technology names rather than brand names, an objective description of methods and results, and no promotional language or comparative claims that the evidence does not support (ISPOR, HEOR impact-case abstract example). The workshop proposal example shows the same standard applied to a session format, structured around a scientific or methodological question rather than a product (ISPOR, workshop proposal example).

A nonpromotional HEOR impact-case abstract typically states:

  • the decision problem or evidence gap;
  • the methods or analysis;
  • the results with appropriate uncertainty;
  • the implication for evidence or decision making,

all in neutral terms. The most common newcomer error is to import commercial framing (brand names, superiority language, selective results); the discipline is to report the analysis as a scientific contribution.

Congress activity involves several asset types and planning steps:

  • Abstract submitted to the congress deadline and, if accepted, developed into a poster or oral presentation.
  • Poster presenting methods and results with the same nonpromotional discipline as the abstract.
  • Oral presentation for accepted work, with speaker preparation and disclosure of conflicts.
  • Encore rules. Re-presentation of previously presented data follows the congress's encore policy and is disclosed as such; it is not passed off as new.

Planning steps include mapping the target congresses to the publication plan, confirming submission and disclosure requirements, and routing every asset through medical, legal, and regulatory review before submission (Module 3).

A congress abstract usually precedes the full manuscript, so the two must be consistent: the abstract should not report a result the eventual paper will not support, and any change between them is explained. Presenters also disclose their conflicts of interest as the society requires, mirroring the disclosure discipline of the manuscript (12.3).

12.6 Compliant medical information responses and enduring materials

Medical information is the function that answers unsolicited questions from healthcare professionals, payers, and patients about a product, including off-label questions that the field must not proactively address (Module 11).

A medical information system typically comprises an inquiry-management process (intake, triage, response, and follow-up), a library of pre-reviewed standard-response documents, and a record of every interaction. Inquiries arrive through multiple channels (telephone, email, congress booths, and field referrals), and each is logged, classified, and answered from an approved source. Any adverse event or product-quality complaint identified during an inquiry is captured and routed under the pharmacovigilance timelines of Module 6.

Compliant response practice rests on three points:

  • Unsolicited request only. A medical information response, including any off-label content, is provided in reaction to a genuine unsolicited request, consistent with the nonpromotional information-exchange principles in Module 3. Pre-approval and post-approval exchange of clinical and economic evidence with payers is subject to its own guidance (AMCP, pre-approval and post-approval clinical and economic evidence guidance).
  • Standard-response documents. Responses are built from pre-reviewed, referenced standard-response documents that present balanced, evidence-based information with citations, so that answers are consistent, accurate, and traceable.
  • Fair balance and referencing. Every response maintains fair balance, cites its evidence, and records the interaction, including any adverse event, which triggers the reporting duty in Module 6.

Medical information activity is measured by responsiveness and by quality (accuracy, fair balance, and traceability of the response), not by volume alone. Inquiry patterns are themselves an insight source: a rising number of unsolicited off-label questions can signal an evidence gap that should feed the integrated evidence generation plan (Module 10), just as a field insight would.

Enduring materials (recorded presentations, slide sets, and written summaries that persist for later use) follow the same nonpromotional, referenced standard and the same medical, legal, and regulatory review as other scientific communications (Module 3).

12.7 Transparency, plain-language summaries, and data sharing

GPP 2022 reflects a broader movement toward transparency in the communication of company-sponsored research beyond the primary manuscript (DeTora et al., 2022). Three practices are now part of a mature communication plan:

  • Plain-language summaries. Accessible summaries of published results for patients and non-specialist readers, written to the same accuracy standard as the manuscript and not used as promotional material.
  • Preprints. Where used, preprints are handled with disclosure of their non-peer-reviewed status and consistency with the eventual peer-reviewed record.
  • Data sharing. Anonymized data and study documents may be shared under governed access, subject to privacy and confidentiality controls (Module 3).

Each of these extends the reach of the scientific record while keeping the same nonpromotional, accountable, and privacy-protected standard that governs the primary publication.

12.8 Publication development and review workflow

Turning a plan into a published paper follows a defined sequence, and each step carries a compliance checkpoint:

  1. nominate authors against the four ICMJE criteria and agree the author group;
  2. review the data and agree an outline with the authors;
  3. draft the manuscript, disclosing any professional medical writing support;
  4. select a legitimate, peer-reviewed journal, screened as in 12.4;
  5. complete internal medical, legal, and regulatory review before submission (Module 3);
  6. submit, respond to peer review, and obtain final approval from all authors;
  7. complete post-publication steps: link the trial registration, prepare a plain-language summary, and retain the records.

The workflow is deliberately author-led rather than sponsor-led at the points that matter for integrity: authors approve the content and are accountable for it, and the sponsor's role is disclosed rather than hidden (DeTora et al., 2022; ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors).

Worked example

Converting an internal HEOR analysis into a nonpromotional abstract

Take an internal HEOR analysis from the capstone scenario, for example a budget-impact or cost-effectiveness result generated in Modules 8 and 9. Using the ISPOR impact-case example as the model, a learner converts it into a submission-ready abstract:

  1. replace the brand name with the generic technology description;
  2. state the decision problem and the analytic method;
  3. report the result with its uncertainty rather than a single favorable figure;
  4. remove any language implying superiority or promotion that the analysis does not support (ISPOR, HEOR impact-case abstract example).

The exercise makes the nonpromotional standard concrete: the same result can be written as a scientific contribution or as a promotional claim, and only the former is submissible. Learners enforce the ISPOR nonpromotional rules in their own draft, as the case-library note for this material requires.

Applied activity

Produces capstone work product 7

Using the assigned capstone product scenario (case J) and the scientific communication case materials (case H), produce two artifacts.

First, a nonpromotional congress abstract in the HEOR impact-case format, built from an HEOR result in the capstone evidence base, using generic technology naming, an objective methods-and-results structure, and no promotional or unsupported comparative language, modeled on the ISPOR example.

Second, a standard medical-information response document for a plausible unsolicited question about the product, presenting balanced, referenced, fair-balanced information with inline citations and a note on adverse-event handling.

The abstract completes capstone work product 7, joining the KOL engagement plan from Module 11; the standard-response document supports the compliance and communication record of the portfolio.

AI-use focus

AI-use focus

Permitted: use an AI assistant to draft sections of an abstract or standard-response document, to triage the literature, and to check readability.

Required controls: the human authors remain fully accountable; every AI-generated statement, statistic, and citation is verified against the primary source before use; AI use is disclosed as the target journal or congress requires, consistent with the ICMJE position that AI cannot be an author and its use must be disclosed (ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors) and with GPP 2022 (DeTora et al., 2022); confidential data is not entered into unvalidated tools; and the use is recorded in the audit log. This applies the program AI-use policy (AI-use policy) and Module 14.

Knowledge check

Answer each question from memory before revealing the model answer. Retrieval practice of this kind improves retention relative to re-reading (Adesope et al., 2017).

1State the ICMJE position on listing an AI tool as an author, and why.Show answer
Model answer

An AI tool cannot be an author because it cannot take responsibility or be accountable for the work; authors must disclose any AI use and remain fully responsible for accuracy and integrity.

2A professional medical writer drafts a manuscript but does not meet all four ICMJE authorship criteria. How is the writer handled?Show answer
Model answer

Not listed as an author; acknowledged as a contributor with the role described and the funding source disclosed. Listing them as an author would be guest authorship; hiding them would be ghostwriting, and both are prohibited.

3Name two requirements GPP 2022 places on a publication plan.Show answer
Model answer

Examples: transparency about the sponsor's role; complete, non-selective reporting of results; authorship aligned to recognized criteria; disclosure of writing support; a documented publication-steering process.

4Give two features that make a congress abstract nonpromotional under the ISPOR standard.Show answer
Model answer

Examples: generic technology names rather than brand names; objective methods-and-results reporting with appropriate uncertainty; absence of superiority or promotional claims not supported by the evidence.

5Under what condition may a medical information team provide off-label information, and from what source?Show answer
Model answer

Only in response to a genuine unsolicited request, using a pre-reviewed, referenced standard-response document that maintains fair balance; the interaction is recorded and any adverse event is reported per Module 6.

6Give two screening checks before selecting a journal to avoid a predatory venue.Show answer

Examples: confirm indexing in a recognized database; verify a described and genuine peer-review process; verify a real editorial board and transparent fees.

7Why is a scientific communication platform useful across functions?Show answer

It states one evidence-based narrative and lexicon so that field medical, publications, congresses, and medical information describe the same data consistently and accurately, reducing the risk of inconsistent or unsupported statements across channels.
