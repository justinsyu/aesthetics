# Module 13: Advisory boards, investigator-sponsored research, and medical education

## Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

1. Design a compliant advisory board with clear objectives and outputs. (D, F)
2. Explain investigator-sponsored and investigator-initiated research governance. (D, F)
3. Describe independent and accredited medical education and the firewall between funding and content. (D, F)

## Why this matters for the career changer

Advisory boards, investigator-sponsored research, and medical education are three of the most useful and most scrutinized activities in medical affairs. Each involves a company paying, or providing something of value to, the same healthcare professionals who prescribe its products. That structure is exactly what anti-kickback and transparency law is designed to police (Module 3).

Each activity is legitimate and valuable when it addresses a genuine scientific or educational need and is documented accordingly; each becomes an inducement, and a legal exposure, when it is a vehicle for reaching prescribers under a scientific label.

A clinician entering industry often has direct experience of these activities from the receiving side, as an advisor, an investigator, or an audience member, and that experience is useful only once it is paired with the compliance discipline that governs the sponsoring side.

The three activities differ chiefly in who leads them, and that difference is the key to their governance. An advisory board is company-convened but expert-advised; an investigator-sponsored study is investigator-led with company support; independent education is provider-controlled with company funding. Getting the leadership-and-control relationship right is what keeps each activity on the correct side of the compliance boundary.

This module builds that discipline for all three activities and produces an advisory board objectives-and-agenda document with a compliant output plan that strengthens the engagement portfolio (capstone work product 7).

## 13.1 Compliant advisory board design

An advisory board convenes external experts to advise the company on a genuine scientific, clinical, or evidence question. The entire compliance posture of an advisory board rests on one prior condition: a genuine, documented need for external advice that the company cannot readily obtain otherwise.

If the company already knows what the meeting will "conclude," or if the real purpose is to expose experts to product messaging or to reward high prescribers, the activity is not an advisory board and should not be run as one.

Advisory boards take several forms, all governed by the same needs-based principle:

- single-meeting boards convened for a specific, time-bound question;
- standing or longitudinal boards that advise over an extended period;
- virtual and hybrid boards;
- disease-state advisory boards focused on the therapeutic area rather than on a single product.

Longer or recurring engagements attract more scrutiny, because repeated payments to the same experts more readily resemble a retainer than needs-based compensation. Each recurrence should be justified by a continuing genuine need.

A defensible advisory board has the following elements:

- **Documented need and objectives.** A written statement of the specific advice sought and why external input is required, framed as scientific questions, not as a communication or sales aim. The number of advisors is the minimum needed to obtain the advice.
- **Needs-based advisor selection.** Advisors are selected for relevant scientific expertise against defined criteria, not for prescribing volume or commercial influence. Selection rationale is documented.
- **An agenda built for input.** The agenda allocates most of the time to discussion and to eliciting advice, not to company presentations. Company content is limited to what the advisors need in order to advise.
- **Documented outputs.** The advice received is captured in a meeting summary or report, and there is a plan for how the input will be used. Outputs that are collected but never used undercut the claim that the advice was genuinely needed.
- **Fair-market-value compensation.** Advisors are paid at fair market value for their time and expertise, documented and reportable under transparency law (Module 3). Payment is for advice rendered, never for prescribing or for any commercial behavior.

Advisory boards are a recognized medical affairs activity whose value depends on disciplined design and on demonstrating impact rather than volume, a theme that runs through the MAPS practice materials on converting operational challenges into strategic contribution ([MAPS, Converting Challenges Into Opportunities](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Challenges-to-Opportunities.pdf); [MAPS/Amedea, innovation and metrics webinar](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/FINAL-SLIDES-MAPS-Webinar-Mar-10-Amedea-Pharma-Final-Updated-for-download14-Read-Only.pdf)).

Governance of these interactions falls within the program's medical governance and compliance domain (Domain F), and advisory activity is a recognized element of launch-phase medical planning ([MAPS, launch-excellence masterclass brochure](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/MAPS-MasterClass-Singapore-English-Brochure-5.13-min.pdf)).

The documentation trail is what makes a compliant advisory board defensible after the fact. A complete record typically includes:

- the written business or scientific need and objectives approved before the meeting;
- the advisor selection criteria and the rationale for each advisor;
- the agenda and pre-read materials;
- the meeting summary capturing the advice received;
- the compensation basis and the fair-market-value determination;
- the record of how the advice was used.

An advisory board that cannot produce these documents is difficult to distinguish, on later review, from a promotional event.

## 13.2 Needs versus promotion: the boundary that defines the activity

The recurring failure mode across all three activities in this module is the same: a promotional purpose dressed as a scientific or educational one. For advisory boards, three questions separate the two:

1. **Is there a genuine question?** If the company is not actually uncertain and does not intend to act on the advice, the meeting is not advisory.
2. **Does the design serve input or output?** Time weighted toward company presentation rather than toward eliciting advice signals a communication event, not an advisory one.
3. **Is compensation tied to advice or to behavior?** Fair-market-value payment for expertise is legitimate; payment that tracks prescribing or that rewards influence is an inducement (Module 3).

The same test applies, with different specifics, to the other two activities:

- For an investigator-sponsored study: is the science genuinely investigator-led and independent, is the support proportional to the work rather than to the investigator's prescribing, and does the company refrain from controlling design, analysis, and publication?
- For independent education: does the accredited provider control the content and faculty, is the grant unrestricted, and is the funding disclosed?

When the honest answer to these questions places an activity on the promotional side, it must either be redesigned to meet a genuine need or run transparently as the promotional activity it is, subject to promotional review; it must not be mislabeled as nonpromotional.

## 13.3 Investigator-sponsored and investigator-initiated research governance

An investigator-sponsored study (ISS), also called an investigator-initiated study, is research conceived and led by an external investigator or institution, for which the company provides support (funding, product, or both) while the investigator, not the company, is the study sponsor and holds regulatory and scientific responsibility.

This inversion of roles is the governance crux: the company must support genuinely independent science without directing it, and without the support functioning as a disguised payment for prescribing.

Companies commonly publish areas of scientific interest that describe the research questions they will consider supporting. These serve two purposes: they help investigators submit relevant proposals, and they keep support decisions anchored to scientific priorities rather than to commercial relationships. A proposal outside the stated areas is not funded merely because the investigator is a valued prescriber.

Compliant ISS governance includes:

- **A defined scientific-merit review.** Proposals are evaluated against transparent scientific criteria and alignment with the company's areas of scientific interest, through a documented review committee separate from commercial decision-making.
- **Clear sponsor-role separation.** The investigator holds sponsor obligations (protocol, conduct, safety reporting, and any registration and disclosure duties); the company's role is limited to the agreed support and does not extend to controlling design, conduct, analysis, or publication.
- **Independence of publication.** The investigator retains the right to publish results, including unfavorable results; the company does not suppress or ghostwrite ISS publications (publication ethics in Module 12).
- **Documentation and fair-market-value support.** The support is documented, justified, provided at fair market value, and reported under transparency law (Module 3); it is never contingent on prescribing.

A concise way to hold the boundary is to separate what the supporting company may and may not do:

- May: fund the study, supply product, review the proposal for scientific merit and safety, and receive results.
- May: decline to support a proposal that lacks scientific merit or falls outside its areas of interest.
- May not: design the protocol, direct the conduct or analysis, control or delay publication, or tie support to prescribing.

The distinction from a company-sponsored study matters for every downstream obligation:

- who registers the trial;
- who reports safety events (Module 6);
- who controls the data and the statistical analysis;
- who holds the right to publish.

Treating an ISS as if it were company-sponsored research, or vice versa, is a governance error with regulatory consequences.

Because the investigator holds sponsor responsibility, safety information generated in an ISS still reaches the company through defined channels so that the company can meet its own pharmacovigilance duties (Module 6), but this exchange does not convert the company into the study sponsor or give it control of the science.

## 13.4 Independent and accredited medical education and the funding-content firewall

It helps to separate three distinct categories that are easily confused:

- **Independent, accredited medical education.** Developed and controlled by an independent, accredited provider; industry may fund it through a grant but does not control content. This is the category the firewall protects.
- **Company-led nonpromotional scientific education.** Organized by the company's medical function; nonpromotional and reviewed, but not independent and not accredited as continuing education.
- **Promotional programs.** For example speaker programs on approved uses; these are promotional activities subject to promotional review and are not education in the independent sense.

Mislabeling one category as another is a common and consequential error. The rest of this section addresses the first category.

Continuing medical education and other independent medical education inform clinical practice and are frequently supported by industry funding. The condition that keeps such support lawful and ethical is a firewall between the funding company and the educational content: the company may provide an unrestricted educational grant, but it must not control, influence, or preview the content, the faculty selection, or the conclusions.

The firewall has practical requirements:

- **Grant, not direction.** Support is provided as an educational grant to an independent provider; the company does not select speakers, shape the agenda, or approve the content of accredited independent education.
- **No content preview or influence.** The company does not review or edit the educational materials of independent programs, and there is documented separation between the personnel who make grant decisions and any commercial interest in the outcome.
- **Transparency.** Funding is disclosed to the audience and reported under transparency law (Module 3).
- **Distinction from company-led education.** Company-organized, nonpromotional scientific education and promotional programs are governed by their own rules (Modules 3 and 12) and must not be presented as independent, accredited education.

The grant-making process itself is governed. Requests are submitted to a review that applies published funding criteria; decisions are made by personnel separated from sales; the educational need and the independence of the provider are documented; and the outcome, including any reconciliation of unspent funds, is recorded. This process is what allows the company to demonstrate, on later review, that it funded education without purchasing influence over it.

Independent education is therefore categorically different from the company's own scientific communications: the value of accredited education to clinicians depends precisely on the company not controlling it. A career changer who treats an educational grant as a communication channel has broken the firewall that gives the education its credibility and its legal standing.

## 13.5 Shared compliance principles across the three activities

Advisory boards, investigator-sponsored research, and independent medical education differ in form, but the compliance principles that govern them are shared. A learner who internalizes these four principles can reason about any new variant of these activities:

- **Genuine need or independence.** The activity must serve a real scientific, evidence, or educational purpose that the company either genuinely needs (advisory boards, ISS) or genuinely does not control (independent education).
- **Selection and support on merit, not on prescribing.** Advisors, investigators, and grant recipients are chosen for expertise and scientific merit, never as a reward for or inducement to prescribe.
- **Fair-market value and transparency.** Anything of value is provided at fair market value, documented, and reported under transparency law (Module 3).
- **Documentation.** The need, the selection rationale, the outputs, and the use of the activity are recorded so that the activity is defensible on later review.

Where an activity fails any of these principles, it is not made compliant by relabeling it; it is either redesigned to meet the principle or run transparently as the promotional activity it is.

## 13.6 Common failure modes

Review committees and compliance functions watch for recurring red flags, each of which converts a legitimate activity into an exposure:

- an advisory board whose agenda is mostly company presentation, with little time for advice;
- advisor or investigator selection that correlates with prescribing volume rather than with expertise;
- output that is collected but never used, or a "need" articulated only after the meeting was booked;
- compensation above fair market value, or payment structured around anything other than the work performed;
- company editing of independent educational content, or company selection of its faculty;
- an investigator-sponsored study in which the company controls the analysis or delays an unfavorable publication;
- any of the above left undocumented.

The remedy in every case is the same: redesign the activity to meet the underlying principle, or reclassify it and run it transparently under the rules that actually apply.

## Worked example: designing an advisory board that meets a genuine need

Using the MAPS practice materials on medical affairs challenges as the framing, take a resource-constrained scenario in which a medical team believes it needs external advice on an evidence gap, for example how clinicians interpret a trial endpoint that payers have questioned ([MAPS, Converting Challenges Into Opportunities](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Challenges-to-Opportunities.pdf)).

The learner works the design decisions in order:

1. state the specific scientific question and confirm the company genuinely needs and will act on the advice;
2. define advisor selection criteria based on relevant expertise rather than prescribing;
3. build an agenda that weights discussion over presentation;
4. specify how the advice will be documented and used;
5. set fair-market-value compensation with a transparency-reporting note.

By contrast, a failed design is easy to recognize in hindsight: an agenda dominated by product slides, an advisor list that maps to the top prescribers, and a meeting "summary" that records agreement with the company's existing position rather than new advice. For each of these, the learner should be able to name which of the three questions in section 13.2 it violates.

The exercise makes the needs-versus-promotion boundary concrete, because at each step there is a legitimate scientific choice and a tempting promotional shortcut, and only the former survives the three questions in section 13.2. Learners produce original design work and do not reproduce the MAPS slides, as the case-library note requires.

## AI-use focus

Permitted: use an AI assistant to synthesize the pre-read literature for an advisory board and to draft a first-pass meeting summary from your own notes.

Required controls: a human validates every synthesized fact and citation against the primary source; advisor identities and any confidential input are not entered into unvalidated tools; AI-drafted summaries are attributed as AI-assisted and confirmed by a named accountable person before circulation; and the use is recorded in the audit log.

Not permitted without human control: generating advisor or investigator selection decisions from an AI ranking without documented human review; attributing AI-drafted content to advisors as if it were their advice; and entering advisor identities, confidential proposals, or unpublished data into a tool that is not validated and approved.

Adoption of these tools in medical affairs is documented but must be governed rather than assumed to be safe ([MAPS, Digital, Advanced Analytics and AI in Medical Affairs benchmark report, 2024](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/MAPS-Digital-Advanced-Analytics-Artificial-Intelligence-Report-2024.pdf)), consistent with current regulatory expectations for documented, risk-based AI governance ([FDA, Considerations for the Use of AI to Support Regulatory Decision-Making (draft, 2025)](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/07/2024-31542/considerations-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-to-support-regulatory-decision-making-for-drug); [EMA, Reflection paper on the use of AI in the medicinal product lifecycle (2024)](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/use-artificial-intelligence-ai-medicinal-product-lifecycle)). ## Knowledge check

1. What single prior condition determines whether an advisory board is compliant, and why? (Answer: a genuine, documented need for external advice that the company intends to act on; without it, the activity is a promotional or inducement vehicle regardless of how it is labeled.)
2. Name three features that distinguish an advisory board from a promotional meeting. (Answer examples: a genuine scientific question the company is uncertain about; an agenda weighted toward eliciting advice rather than company presentation; advisor selection and fair-market-value payment tied to expertise and advice, not to prescribing.)
3. In an investigator-sponsored study, who holds sponsor responsibility, and what does that imply for publication? (Answer: the investigator or institution is the sponsor and holds regulatory and scientific responsibility, including safety reporting and registration; the investigator retains the right to publish results, including unfavorable ones, and the company does not control design, analysis, or publication.)
4. Describe the funding-content firewall in independent medical education. (Answer: the company may provide an unrestricted educational grant but must not control or preview content, faculty, or conclusions; grant decisions are separated from commercial interest, and funding is disclosed and reported.)
5. Why must fair-market-value compensation and transparency reporting apply across all three activities? (Answer: each provides value to prescribers, so payment above fair market value or tied to prescribing is an inducement under anti-kickback law, and payments are reportable under transparency law; see Module 3.)
6. An educational grant recipient asks the funding company to approve the slide deck before the accredited program. What is the correct response? (Answer: decline; reviewing or approving content of independent accredited education breaks the funding-content firewall and removes the independence that makes the education legitimate.)
7. Why does collecting advisory board output but never using it weaken the compliance position? (Answer: it undercuts the claim that external advice was genuinely needed and acted on, which is the prior condition that distinguishes an advisory board from a promotional or inducement activity.)
8. Distinguish independent accredited medical education from company-led nonpromotional scientific education. (Answer: independent accredited education is developed and controlled by an independent accredited provider that the company may fund but not direct; company-led scientific education is organized and controlled by the company's medical function, is nonpromotional and reviewed, but is neither independent nor accredited, and it must not be presented as if it were.)
9. Why does a standing advisory board attract more scrutiny than a single-meeting board? (Answer: recurring payments to the same experts more readily resemble a retainer than needs-based compensation, so each recurrence must be justified by a continuing genuine need and documented accordingly.)
