# Module 6: Pharmacovigilance, drug safety, and risk management

Domains B and F (Scientific and clinical foundations; Medical governance and compliance). Approximately 5 hours.

## Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

1. Describe pharmacovigilance systems, adverse-event reporting, and signal detection at a working level. (B, F)
2. Explain benefit-risk assessment and risk management, including Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS). (B, F)
3. Describe the medical affairs and MSL responsibilities in safety reporting and the timelines that govern them. (F)

## Why this matters for the career changer

A clinician who observes an adverse reaction manages the patient and, ideally, reports it. An industry professional sits inside the system that receives, evaluates, and acts on those reports across an entire product's life.

Two shifts of perspective matter for the career changer. First, safety is not a fixed property established at approval; the benefit-risk balance is provisional and is reassessed continuously as exposure grows, which is why approvals on incomplete evidence (Module 5) carry heightened surveillance obligations.

Second, everyone in a field-medical or medical-information role is part of the reporting system. An adverse event mentioned in a scientific exchange is not an aside to be noted later; it is information the individual has a defined duty to capture and route.

This module describes pharmacovigilance concepts at a working level and builds the reporting reflexes and documentation habits that a compliant field-medical professional needs. It produces an adverse-event handling flow that is incorporated into the capstone field-medical work (work product 7).

The concepts here are deliberately at a principle level. Specific timelines, report formats, and statutory requirements vary by jurisdiction and are set by ICH and regional regulation; where this module states such a specific requirement, it flags the need to verify it against the governing source rather than asserting a number.

## 6.1 The purpose and structure of pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance is the set of activities concerned with detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects and other drug-related problems across a product's life.

Premarketing trials, however well designed, enroll limited numbers of relatively selected participants over limited time. Rare, delayed, and population-specific harms often become detectable only with broad, prolonged, real-world exposure. Pharmacovigilance therefore extends the safety inquiry that began in development (Module 4) into the entire post-approval period.

Pharmacovigilance is a recognized competency within the medical affairs knowledge base ([ACMA, BCMAS Program Information](https://medicalaffairsspecialist.org/certifications/bcmas/program-info)), and continued safety evidence generation is one strand of the integrated, lifecycle view of evidence that the curriculum treats as a spine ([Schneeweiss and Miksad, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.3603)).

## 6.2 Adverse-event definitions and seriousness

Precise terminology is the basis of consistent reporting.

- An **adverse event (AE)** is any untoward medical occurrence in a patient administered a product, whether or not it is considered related to the product.
- An **adverse drug reaction (ADR)** is an adverse event for which a causal relationship to the product is at least a reasonable possibility.
- **Seriousness** is a regulatory classification, distinct from severity (which describes intensity). An event is generally classified as serious if it results in death, is life-threatening, requires or prolongs hospitalization, causes persistent or significant disability or incapacity, is a congenital anomaly, or is another medically important event. A mild event can be serious by these criteria, and a severe event may not be.
- **Expectedness** describes whether an event is consistent with the existing safety information for the product. An event that is both serious and unexpected carries the most stringent reporting expectations.

These definitions are standardized internationally through the ICH pharmacovigilance guidelines (the E2 series) ([ICH, Efficacy Guidelines](https://www.ich.org/page/efficacy-guidelines)). They are described here at a principle level; any specific regulatory definition applied in practice should be verified against the governing ICH and regional references.

## 6.3 Individual case safety reports and reporting timelines

The individual case safety report (ICSR) is the unit of adverse-event reporting. A valid case generally requires four minimum elements:

1. An identifiable reporter.
2. An identifiable patient.
3. A suspect product.
4. An adverse event.

Cases missing an element are followed up to obtain it rather than discarded.

Reports move on defined clocks. Serious and unexpected reactions are subject to expedited reporting on short timelines (commonly within a small fixed number of calendar days of a company becoming aware of the case), while other events are aggregated into periodic reports. The exact windows, definitions, and standards for managing and reporting individual case safety reports are set out in the ICH E2D guideline and adopting regional guidance, and must be verified against those sources rather than assumed ([FDA, E2D(R1) Postapproval Safety Data: Definitions and Standards for Management and Reporting of Individual Case Safety Reports](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/e2dr1-post-approval-safety-data-definitions-and-standards-management-and-reporting-individual-case)).

The operational point for a field professional is that the clock starts when any company representative becomes aware of a reportable case. That is precisely why prompt internal routing matters (section 6.8).

## 6.4 Signal detection

A safety signal is information suggesting a new potential causal association, or a new aspect of a known association, that warrants further evaluation. Signals arise from spontaneous report databases, from the literature, from clinical studies, and increasingly from real-world data.

Detection combines quantitative screening with clinical judgment. Disproportionality analyses flag product-event combinations reported more often than expected in a spontaneous-reporting database; such a flag is a hypothesis to be evaluated, not a confirmed causal finding. The appraisal cautions from Module 4 (confounding, bias, and multiplicity) apply to signal evaluation as they do to any analysis.

When a signal is prioritized, it is evaluated formally: the evidence for and against a causal association is weighed, and, if the association is supported, the response can include a labeling change, a communication to prescribers, an added risk-management measure, or, in rare cases, market withdrawal. Signal management is the bridge between detection and action.

## 6.5 Periodic safety reporting

Aggregate safety experience is summarized in periodic reports over a product's life, for investigational products during development and for authorized products after approval. These periodic assessments integrate accumulating data to re-examine whether the safety profile and the benefit-risk balance remain as characterized.

The specific report types, their contents, and their periodicity are defined by ICH and regional requirements, such as the European Union good pharmacovigilance practices modules, and should be cited to those references when stated precisely ([EMA, Good pharmacovigilance practices (GVP)](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/post-authorisation/pharmacovigilance-post-authorisation/good-pharmacovigilance-practices-gvp)). The principle to retain is that safety is reassessed on a schedule, not only when a single dramatic case appears.

## 6.6 Benefit-risk assessment and its reassessment across the lifecycle

Regulatory decisions rest on a benefit-risk judgment: the favorable effects of a product weighed against its harms for a defined population and use. That judgment is not settled at approval.

As exposure accumulates, new harms may emerge, the magnitude of benefit in practice may differ from the trial estimate, and the therapeutic context may change. Structured benefit-risk frameworks make the weighing explicit by identifying the relevant benefits and risks, their magnitude and uncertainty, and the value judgments applied, so that reassessment is transparent and reproducible.

A structured assessment typically makes explicit:

- The therapeutic context and the available alternatives.
- The key benefits, with their magnitude and uncertainty.
- The key risks, with their magnitude and uncertainty.
- The value trade-offs applied in weighing benefits against risks.
- The residual uncertainty, and what new evidence would change the conclusion.

Products approved on incomplete evidence, such as an accelerated approval on a surrogate endpoint (Module 5), begin their marketed life with more residual uncertainty and therefore require more active post-approval evidence generation to resolve it ([Vreman, Leufkens, and Kesselheim, 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2020.569535)).

## 6.7 Risk management and REMS

When routine labeling and pharmacovigilance are not sufficient to ensure that a product's benefits outweigh its risks, regulators can require a risk-management program. In the United States this is a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS): a required program whose elements are scaled to the risk.

- A **medication guide or patient package insert** communicates specific risks to patients.
- A **communication plan** conveys risk information to healthcare providers.
- **Elements to assure safe use** apply to the highest-risk situations and can include prescriber certification, restricted dispensing, or documentation of safe-use conditions.
- An **implementation system and assessment** monitor whether the REMS is working and is reassessed over time.

The purpose is to enable access to a product whose risks would otherwise be difficult to manage, by adding targeted controls proportional to the risk. The statutory basis, the defined REMS elements, and the assessment requirements are set out in FDA regulations and guidance and should be cited to those primary sources when stated precisely ([FDA, Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (REMS)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/risk-evaluation-and-mitigation-strategies-rems)). Analogous risk-management planning exists in other jurisdictions, for example the risk-management plan under the European Union good pharmacovigilance practices ([EMA, Good pharmacovigilance practices (GVP)](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/post-authorisation/pharmacovigilance-post-authorisation/good-pharmacovigilance-practices-gvp)).

## 6.8 The field-medical duty to report

Field-medical and medical-information professionals routinely encounter adverse-event information in the course of scientific exchange, medical inquiries, and congress interactions. The governing principle is straightforward: an MSL or medical-information specialist who becomes aware of a reportable adverse event has a defined duty to capture and route it to the company's safety function within the required internal timeline, regardless of whether the interaction concerned safety.

The field professional's obligations are to:

- Recognize a potential reportable case.
- Record the minimum case elements (identifiable reporter, patient, suspect product, and event).
- Report it promptly through the designated internal channel.
- Refrain from offering clinical management advice beyond the approved label or the scope of the role.
- Document the interaction so that it satisfies both the safety-reporting duty and the nonpromotional standard.

This duty coexists with the nonpromotional boundary (Module 3): the same interaction that must stay within nonpromotional scientific exchange also carries a mandatory safety-reporting obligation. Field-medical deployment models and the MSL role are described in the industry literature ([IQVIA, Medical Science Liaisons white paper](https://www.iqvia.com/-/media/library/white-papers/medical-science-liaisons.pdf)), and scenario-based safety and medical-affairs training reinforces the reporting reflex ([DIA, Medical Affairs eLearning Program](https://www.diaglobal.org/en/course-listing/elearning/modules/medical-affairs)).

## 6.9 Safety in real-world data

Real-world data sources (claims, electronic health records, registries, and patient-generated data, developed in Module 7) increasingly support post-approval safety evaluation and signal assessment. They expand the population and duration under observation.

They also carry the validity threats of any observational source (confounding, selection, and misclassification), so a real-world safety signal is evaluated with the same caution as any observational finding. Continued, structured safety and effectiveness evidence generation after launch is part of the integrated evidence view ([Schneeweiss and Miksad, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.3603)).

## 6.10 The pharmacovigilance system and defined responsibilities

A marketing-authorization holder maintains a pharmacovigilance system: the structures, processes, and people that carry out safety activities across a product's life. Core components include a validated safety database, written standard operating procedures, trained staff, and defined accountability for safety decisions.

Many jurisdictions require the holder to designate a specific person responsible for pharmacovigilance and to describe the system in a master file; the precise titles, obligations, and documentation requirements are set by regional regulation, such as the European Union good pharmacovigilance practices, and should be cited to those sources when stated precisely ([EMA, Good pharmacovigilance practices (GVP)](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/human-regulatory-overview/post-authorisation/pharmacovigilance-post-authorisation/good-pharmacovigilance-practices-gvp)).

For a field-medical professional, the relevance is practical: the system defines where a report must go, how quickly, and who is accountable, and it is the reason individual routing duties (section 6.8) are specified rather than left to judgment.

## 6.11 Sources of safety data across the lifecycle

Safety information reaches the system from several channels, each with different completeness and susceptibility to bias:

- **Spontaneous reports** from clinicians, patients, and others; timely but subject to under-reporting and incomplete detail.
- **Solicited reports** from clinical trials, registries, and organized data-collection or patient-support programs; more complete but drawn from selected populations.
- **Literature** surveillance for published case reports and studies.
- **Real-world data** sources (section 6.9), which extend observation but carry observational validity threats.

A field professional most often generates a spontaneous or solicited report; recognizing which channel an interaction belongs to helps route it correctly and set expectations about how complete the initial information will be.

## 6.12 Data quality, coding, and case follow-up

Aggregate safety analysis is only as reliable as the individual cases that feed it, so case management includes several quality steps.

- **Follow-up.** Initial reports are often incomplete; structured follow-up obtains missing clinical detail and the outcome.
- **De-duplication.** The same case can arrive through more than one channel and must not be counted twice.
- **Medical coding.** Events are coded with standard medical terminology so that similar events can be grouped for signal detection (section 6.4).
- **Causality and seriousness assessment.** Each case is assessed against defined criteria, with documented reasoning.

For a field professional, the practical contribution is a complete, accurate initial report: the more precisely the event, product, timing, and outcome are captured at the source, the more reliable every downstream analysis becomes.

## 6.13 Constraints on AI in pharmacovigilance

Artificial intelligence is being applied to case processing, literature screening, and signal detection, but pharmacovigilance is a setting where the program AI-use controls are strict.

Automated tools may triage and draft, but adjudication of causality, seriousness, and reportability requires validated methods and documented human sign-off, and case data are confidential and must be handled accordingly.

These constraints align with how regulators frame AI use across the medicinal product lifecycle ([EMA, reflection paper on the use of AI in the medicinal product lifecycle](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/use-artificial-intelligence-ai-medicinal-product-lifecycle)) and with the risk-based, context-of-use approach in the FDA's draft framework for AI in regulatory decision-making ([FDA, considerations for the use of AI to support regulatory decision-making, Federal Register notice, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/07/2024-31542/considerations-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-to-support-regulatory-decision-making-for-drug)).

## Worked example: safety across the lifecycle of a rare-disease product

Use the FDA LEADER 3D tofersen case as the product context ([FDA, LEADER 3D tofersen case](https://www.fda.gov/media/186135/download?attachment=)).

Because the teaching scenario involves a surrogate-based accelerated pathway in a small population (Module 5), the marketed product begins with substantial residual uncertainty about its clinical benefit and long-term safety, which is precisely the condition under which pharmacovigilance and benefit-risk reassessment must be most active ([Vreman, Leufkens, and Kesselheim, 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2020.569535)).

Now place a field-medical professional in this setting: during a scientific exchange, a treating physician mentions that a patient experienced a serious adverse event. The learner traces the required response:

- Recognize the reportable case.
- Capture the four minimum elements.
- Route it to the safety function within the internal timeline.
- Avoid giving clinical management advice beyond the label.
- Document the interaction consistently with the nonpromotional boundary (Module 3).

A further step is to consider whether a product with this risk profile would warrant a formal risk-management program (section 6.7) and how a field team would support any safe-use communication within the nonpromotional boundary.

The exercise integrates the lifecycle-safety concept with the field-medical duty to report ([IQVIA, Medical Science Liaisons white paper](https://www.iqvia.com/-/media/library/white-papers/medical-science-liaisons.pdf)). FDA states the LEADER 3D cases are teaching scenarios assessed case by case, not approval templates ([FDA, LEADER 3D program](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/accelerating-rare-disease-cures-arc-program/learning-and-education-advance-and-empower-rare-disease-drug-developers-leader-3d)).

## AI-use focus

Permitted: use an AI assistant to draft the structure of an adverse-event handling flow or a training aid, and to screen literature for potential safety information.

Required controls: do not use AI to adjudicate causality, seriousness, or reportability without validated methods and documented human sign-off, because an incorrect automated classification can cause a reportable case to be missed or misrouted; never enter identifiable case data or confidential safety information into a tool that is not validated and approved; preserve the human duty to report on the required timeline; record the AI use in your audit log (tool, task, what you verified, who is accountable). These constraints follow the program AI-use policy (`governance/ai_use_policy_and_playbook.md`) and current regulatory thinking on AI in the product lifecycle ([EMA, reflection paper on AI](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/use-artificial-intelligence-ai-medicinal-product-lifecycle); [FDA, AI in regulatory decision-making, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/07/2024-31542/considerations-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-to-support-regulatory-decision-making-for-drug)).

## Knowledge check

1. Distinguish seriousness from severity. (Answer: seriousness is a regulatory classification based on defined outcomes such as death, hospitalization, or disability; severity describes intensity. A mild event can be serious, and a severe event may not meet the seriousness criteria.)
2. What are the minimum elements of a valid individual case safety report? (Answer: an identifiable reporter, an identifiable patient, a suspect product, and an adverse event; missing elements are pursued through follow-up.)
3. Why is a disproportionality flag a hypothesis rather than a conclusion? (Answer: it indicates a product-event combination reported more often than expected, but reporting patterns are subject to confounding and bias; causality requires clinical evaluation.)
4. Why does an accelerated approval increase the pharmacovigilance burden? (Answer: approval on a surrogate leaves residual uncertainty about clinical benefit and long-term safety, which must be resolved by active post-approval evidence generation and benefit-risk reassessment.)
5. When may a REMS include elements to assure safe use, and what is its purpose? (Answer: when routine labeling and pharmacovigilance are insufficient to keep benefits ahead of risks; the purpose is to enable access while adding controls proportional to the risk. Verify the statutory elements against FDA sources.)
6. An MSL learns of a serious adverse event during a nonpromotional scientific exchange. What are the obligations? (Answer: recognize the reportable case, capture the minimum elements, route it to the safety function within the internal timeline, avoid clinical advice beyond the label, and document within the nonpromotional boundary.)
7. Why is a real-world safety signal evaluated with the same caution as any observational finding? (Answer: real-world sources carry confounding, selection, and misclassification; the expanded exposure they offer does not remove these validity threats.)
8. What is signal management, and what can it lead to? (Answer: the formal evaluation of a prioritized signal, weighing the evidence for and against a causal association; a supported association can lead to a labeling change, a prescriber communication, an added risk-management measure, or, rarely, market withdrawal.)
9. Why does the quality of an initial field report matter for the whole system? (Answer: aggregate analyses and signal detection depend on complete, accurate, de-duplicated, and consistently coded cases; an incomplete source report degrades every downstream assessment.)
