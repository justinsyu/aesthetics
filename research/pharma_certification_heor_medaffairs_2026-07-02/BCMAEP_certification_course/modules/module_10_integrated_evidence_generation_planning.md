# Module 10: Integrated evidence generation planning (IEGP)

Domains C and G (Evidence generation, HEOR, HTA, and market access; Strategy, leadership, and evidence-based decision making). Approximately 8 hours.

## Learning objectives

On completion, the learner can:

1. Define integrated evidence generation planning and explain how it aligns clinical development, medical affairs, HEOR, market access, and regulatory evidence needs across the lifecycle. (C, G)
2. Conduct an evidence-gap assessment and translate stakeholder evidence needs into prioritized evidence questions. (C, G)
3. Build an integrated evidence matrix mapping questions to study concepts, data sources, timing, owners, and decision points. (C, G)
4. Define evidence-impact metrics and a governance model for plan maintenance. (C, G)

## Why this matters for the career changer

Every prior module in Domain C produced one piece of an evidence base: a real-world study (Module 7), an economic model (Module 8), an HTA or payer submission (Module 9). Left uncoordinated, these pieces are generated by different functions, on different timelines, to answer different questions, and they arrive too late or answer the wrong question for the decision at hand.

Integrated evidence generation planning is the discipline that puts them on one map. It starts from the decisions that matter (a regulatory filing, an HTA appraisal, a formulary review, a guideline update, an internal go/no-go) and works backward to the evidence each decision requires, then assigns each evidence question a study concept, a data source, a timeline, an owner, and a metric for whether it changed the decision.

For a career changer, this is the module where the separate methods become a strategy. It is also the signature deliverable of this program: the integrated evidence generation plan is a core capstone component and the artifact that most directly demonstrates the ability to think across development, medical affairs, HEOR, and access at once. A clinician is trained to consume evidence; this module trains the professional to plan its generation.

The shift is not only organizational but temporal. Evidence decisions made years apart, a trial endpoint chosen in early development and a comparator demanded by a payer at launch, are connected: the first constrains what the second can be answered with. Integrated evidence planning is the practice of seeing those connections in advance and acting on them while the studies that could satisfy them are still open.

## 10.1 What integrated evidence generation planning is and why it exists

Integrated evidence generation planning is a cross-functional process that identifies the evidence a product will need across its lifecycle, prioritizes it against the decisions it must inform, and coordinates its generation so that the right evidence is available at the right time for the right stakeholder. It exists because the alternative, siloed study planning, systematically produces gaps and duplication: clinical development optimizes for the registration dossier, HEOR generates value evidence on a separate cycle, and medical affairs collects real-world and post-launch evidence after commitments have already hardened. Each function can execute well and the product can still arrive at an HTA appraisal missing the comparator the committee needs.

The case for integration is now made explicitly in the literature. Schneeweiss and Miksad describe integrated evidence generation as a single view of a medication's evidence needs that runs from early development through payer and budget decisions, coordinating clinical trials, real-world evidence, and economic analysis rather than treating them as sequential handoffs ([Schneeweiss and Miksad, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.3603)). Integrated evidence planning and the identification of data gaps are increasingly recognized as named competencies for medical affairs and evidence roles, and this program develops them alongside HEOR and real-world evidence.

The core move is to treat evidence generation as a continuous, owned plan rather than a series of studies. Three shifts distinguish the integrated approach from siloed planning:

- **From study-first to decision-first.** The unit of planning is the decision to be informed, not the study to be run; studies are the means, not the object.
- **From functional to cross-functional ownership.** One plan is owned jointly by clinical development, medical affairs, HEOR, regulatory, and market access, rather than a set of plans owned separately.
- **From static to continuous.** The plan is revised as evidence accrues and decisions approach, rather than fixed at a single planning cycle.

| Dimension | Siloed study planning | Integrated evidence generation planning |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of planning | The study | The decision to be informed |
| Ownership | Each function plans its own studies | One plan owned jointly across functions |
| Timing logic | Studies scheduled by functional cycle | Studies scheduled backward from the decision |
| Typical failure | Gaps and duplication discovered late | Gaps surfaced and prioritized in advance |
| Success measure | Studies completed | Decisions informed |

Schneeweiss and Miksad make the same point in lifecycle terms: the value of a medication is decided in stages, from the regulatory filing through the payer and budget decision, and evidence planned for only the first stage leaves the later decisions under-served ([Schneeweiss and Miksad, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.3603)). The integrated plan treats the whole sequence of decisions as the object of planning from the outset.

## 10.2 Stakeholder evidence needs

An integrated plan begins by naming the stakeholders whose decisions the product must satisfy and stating what each one needs. The needs differ, and a study designed for one stakeholder rarely satisfies another without deliberate design.

- **Regulators** need evidence that the product is safe and effective for a defined use: pivotal efficacy and safety data and, after approval, any confirmatory or safety commitments. Getting this evidence right after approval is itself a planning problem, because post-approval commitments are frequently under-specified at launch and then generate evidence that does not resolve the questions decision-makers actually have ([Vreman et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2020.569535)).
- **HTA bodies and payers** need comparative effectiveness against the relevant comparator, cost-effectiveness, and budget impact, prepared to a recognized reporting standard so a committee can appraise it ([Husereau et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-021-07460-7)). The comparator and outcomes they care about are often not those of the pivotal trial (Module 9), which is a predictable and therefore plannable evidence gap.
- **Clinicians** need evidence that applies to the patients they treat: effectiveness in routine populations, comparative safety, adherence and persistence, and the therapy's place relative to alternatives. Evidence confined to a narrow trial population may not answer their question.
- **Patients and caregivers** need evidence on outcomes that matter to them, including symptoms, function, and quality of life, and are increasingly involved in defining which outcomes a development program should measure ([EUPATI, guidance for patient involvement in industry-led medicines R&D](https://toolbox.eupati.eu/resources/patient-toolbox/guidance-for-patient-involvement-in-industry-led-medicines-rd/)).

The following summary makes the differing needs and the predictable gaps explicit; the sourced claims are cited in the bullets above.

| Stakeholder | Decision it informs | Evidence it needs | Typical gap at launch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulators | Approval and post-approval commitments | Pivotal efficacy and safety in the defined use | Confirmatory or long-term safety evidence under-specified |
| HTA bodies and payers | Coverage, pricing, reimbursement | Comparative effectiveness and cost-effectiveness against the relevant comparator | No head-to-head data against the payer's comparator |
| Clinicians | Whether and when to use the therapy | Effectiveness and safety in routine patients; place in therapy | Evidence confined to a narrow trial population |
| Patients and caregivers | Whether the therapy fits their goals | Outcomes that matter to them (symptoms, function, quality of life) | Patient-relevant outcomes not measured as endpoints |

Writing these needs down as distinct is the first discipline of the plan. It exposes two things at once: where a single well-designed study can serve several stakeholders (a trial that measures a patient-relevant outcome against an active comparator can serve regulators, payers, clinicians, and patients together), and where a stakeholder's decision has no evidence assigned to it at all.

## 10.3 Evidence-gap and landscape assessment

The evidence-gap assessment compares the evidence each stakeholder decision requires against the evidence that exists or is already planned. It has three steps.

1. **Map the landscape.** Catalogue the current evidence to establish what is already known, what competitors have shown, and where the field is moving. A landscape scan typically covers:
   - the product's own completed and ongoing trials and their endpoints;
   - the published literature on the condition and the drug class;
   - the competitor evidence base, including comparators and outcomes studied;
   - treatment guidelines and the outcomes they weight; and
   - relevant HTA and payer precedents in the target markets.
2. **Specify the decisions and their evidence requirements.** For each stakeholder decision (a filing, an HTA appraisal, a formulary review, a guideline update, an internal investment decision), state precisely what evidence would satisfy it: the population, comparator, outcome, time horizon, and level of certainty required.
3. **Identify the gaps.** A gap is the difference between requirement and reality. Common categories recur across products:
   - **Comparator gaps:** the payer's comparator was never studied head to head.
   - **Population gaps:** a subgroup that will be treated (older, comorbid, pediatric) has no data.
   - **Outcome gaps:** a patient-relevant or payer-relevant outcome was not measured.
   - **Surrogate-acceptance gaps:** the trial endpoint is a surrogate a decision-maker will not accept without a link to a final outcome.
   - **Time-horizon gaps:** the decision needs longer-term evidence than any trial provides.
   - **Data-source gaps:** no fit-for-purpose registry or database yet exists to answer the question.

Vreman and colleagues note that these gaps are often visible before approval but are addressed only afterward, if at all, which is why prospective planning matters more than reactive study commissioning ([Vreman et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2020.569535)). A gap is worth acting on only if closing it would change a decision. The assessment therefore records, for each gap, which decision it blocks and how large the consequence is if it remains open. This framing carries directly into prioritization, and it is what separates a genuine gap assessment from an inventory of everything not yet studied.

## 10.4 Prioritization

An evidence-gap assessment typically surfaces more questions than any program can fund. Prioritization ranks them so that finite resources answer the questions that most change decisions. Useful criteria include:

- **Decision impact.** Would answering this question change a regulatory, HTA, payer, guideline, or clinical decision, and how consequential is that decision for access and appropriate use?
- **Uncertainty.** How uncertain is the current answer, and would the study meaningfully reduce that uncertainty rather than confirm what is already accepted?
- **Feasibility and timing.** Can the evidence be generated in time for the decision it must inform, and is a valid data source or study design available to answer it?
- **Cost and opportunity cost.** What does the study cost, and what evidence is displaced by funding it?

A simple way to make this explicit is to rate each candidate question on decision impact and on the reducible uncertainty, and to fund first the questions that are high on both and feasible in the required window. The illustration below shows how a short list might be ranked; the ratings are judgments made by the cross-functional group, not fixed values.

| Candidate evidence question | Decision impact | Reducible uncertainty | Feasible in window | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Comparative effectiveness versus the payer's comparator | High | High | Yes | Fund first |
| Long-term durability beyond the trial horizon | High | Moderate | Only via post-launch data | Fund, sequenced later |
| Effectiveness in a small comorbid subgroup | Moderate | High | Yes | Fund if resources allow |
| Confirmatory replication of an already-accepted effect | Low | Low | Yes | Defer or drop |

The output is a ranked set of evidence questions, each tied to the decision it serves. Because most candidate molecules and most evidence questions cannot all be pursued, prioritization is not administrative; it is where the plan's strategy is set. The severity of a gap, the strength of the decision it blocks, and the achievability of a credible answer together determine what enters the matrix and what is deferred or dropped.

Prioritization is also a sequencing problem, not only a selection problem. Some high-impact evidence cannot be generated before launch because it depends on routine use (long-term durability, effectiveness in unselected patients, adherence in practice), so it is scheduled for the post-launch period and tied to a later decision such as a payer re-review or a guideline update. A useful plan therefore separates the evidence needed for the first access decision from the evidence that will strengthen or defend the position later, and assigns each to the decision it can realistically inform.

## 10.5 The integrated evidence matrix

The integrated evidence matrix is the operational core of the plan. It converts each prioritized evidence question into a committed, ownable line of work. A workable matrix carries at least these columns:

- **Evidence question.** The specific question a stakeholder decision requires, stated with its population, comparator, and outcome, so it is answerable rather than a theme.
- **Study concept.** The design that would answer it: a randomized trial, a single-arm study, a real-world comparative study, an indirect treatment comparison, an economic model, a patient-preference or patient-reported-outcome study, or a targeted literature synthesis.
- **Data source.** Where the evidence comes from: a specific trial, a registry, a claims or electronic-health-record database, primary data collection, or an existing published set. Naming the source forces a feasibility check and prevents a study concept with no viable data behind it.
- **Timing.** When the evidence is needed, anchored to the decision it informs, and when the study must therefore start and read out. Timing is a constraint, not a wish: a study that reads out after the decision it was meant to inform has no impact.
- **Owner.** The single accountable function or role (clinical development, HEOR, medical affairs, epidemiology, regulatory, market access), so that no line is unowned and no line has diffuse ownership.
- **Decision point.** The specific decision the evidence feeds: a regulatory filing, an HTA appraisal, a formulary review, a guideline submission, a managed-entry data commitment, or an internal go/no-go.
- **Impact metric.** How the plan will know the evidence mattered: whether it was cited in the decision, changed a recommendation, resolved a stated uncertainty, or supported a coverage or pricing outcome.

Designing study concepts to be fit for purpose is where the earlier modules attach. A real-world comparative concept should be framed as a target-trial emulation so its assumptions are explicit and defensible to a critical committee ([Hernán and Robins, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwv254)); an economic concept should be planned from the outset to report against CHEERS so it is appraisable ([Husereau et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-021-07460-7)); and an HTA-facing concept should target the comparator and outcomes the appraisal will actually use (Module 9). The matrix is the single document in which those choices are made once and coordinated across functions, rather than rediscovered independently by each.

Matching the study concept to the evidence question is a recurring judgment. The mapping below is a starting point, not a rule; the choice always depends on feasibility, timing, and the level of certainty the decision requires.

| Type of evidence question | Typical study concept | Typical data source |
|---|---|---|
| Efficacy versus placebo in a defined population | Randomized controlled trial | Primary trial data |
| Relative effect versus an unstudied comparator | Indirect treatment comparison or network meta-analysis | Published trial network |
| Effectiveness in routine care and excluded subgroups | Real-world comparative study (target-trial emulation) | Claims or electronic-health-record data |
| Cost-effectiveness and budget impact | Decision-analytic model reported to CHEERS | Trial effects, utilities, local costs |
| Outcomes that matter to patients | Patient-preference or patient-reported-outcome study | Primary data collection with patients |

The evidence matrix is one component of the plan document, not the whole of it. A complete integrated evidence generation plan typically contains: a short statement of the product and its lifecycle position; the stakeholder evidence needs; the evidence-gap assessment and the prioritization behind it; the matrix itself; the linkage to the dynamic target product profile; the governance model and review cadence; and a cross-reference to the AI-use log for any AI-assisted step. The applied activity below assembles these components.

## 10.6 Global, regional, and local alignment

Evidence needs are not uniform across markets. A global plan sets the core evidence strategy and the studies that serve every market; regional and local (affiliate) plans add the evidence a specific HTA body, payer, or guideline requires. The alignment problem is to let local needs shape the plan without fragmenting it into inconsistent one-off studies.

Two design rules keep the plan coherent:

- **Generate globally usable evidence once.** A study designed with the outcomes, comparators, and reporting standards that the major HTA bodies share can serve many submissions, whereas a study designed for a single market often cannot transfer; the transferability constraints in Module 9 apply directly, so a global study should anticipate the strictest common requirement.
- **Record local commitments in the same matrix as global studies.** An affiliate's managed-access data requirement or a local comparator study must be visible to the global owner, so that it neither duplicates a global study nor contradicts the core evidence, and so that a local commitment made in a pricing negotiation is resourced rather than orphaned.

The launch-excellence literature frames this cross-market coordination as a defining task of the launch period, when regulatory, HTA, and field-medical evidence needs converge on the same product at once and a plan that is coherent globally but silent on local requirements will fail in individual markets ([MAPS, launch-excellence masterclass brochure](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/MAPS-MasterClass-Singapore-English-Brochure-5.13-min.pdf)).

A workable division of responsibility keeps global and local evidence coherent:

| Level | What it owns | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Global | Core evidence strategy and studies serving all markets | Pivotal trials; a globally usable comparative and economic base |
| Regional | Evidence shared across markets in a region | A network meta-analysis against a regionally common comparator |
| Local (affiliate) | Evidence a specific HTA body or payer requires | A local-cost economic adaptation; a managed-access data commitment |

The recording rule matters as much as the division: a local commitment made in a pricing negotiation belongs in the same matrix as the global studies, with an owner and a deadline, so that it is resourced and does not silently duplicate or contradict the core evidence.

## 10.7 The dynamic target product profile

The target product profile (TPP) states the product's intended profile, the claims the developer aims to support, and the evidence required to support them. Treated as a static regulatory document, it captures only the label. Treated as a dynamic tool, it becomes the bridge between the evidence plan and the value proposition: it is updated as evidence accrues, and it carries the HTA and payer requirements into development decisions from the start.

Wang and colleagues describe the dynamic TPP as a mechanism for building HTA and payer perspectives into drug development, so that the comparator, outcomes, and subgroups that access decisions will require are designed into the evidence base rather than retrofitted after approval ([Wang et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2022.948161)). A TPP used this way typically records, for each attribute:

- the target level the developer aims to demonstrate (for example a magnitude of effect on a specified outcome);
- the minimum acceptable level below which the value proposition fails;
- the comparator against which the attribute will be judged; and
- the evidence, and the matrix line, that will substantiate it.

Used this way, the TPP and the integrated evidence matrix are two views of the same plan: the TPP states the claims and the value target, and the matrix states the studies that will substantiate them. When a study reads out or a gap is closed, both are updated together, so the value proposition stays tied to the evidence that supports it and never drifts ahead of it.

The dynamic TPP also disciplines the difference between an aspiration and a substantiated claim. A claim in the TPP that has no line in the matrix is an aspiration without an evidence route; a line in the matrix that maps to no claim is a study without a purpose. Reconciling the two is a recurring governance task.

Reading the TPP forward and backward makes the plan self-checking. Read forward, each intended claim raises the question of what evidence would substantiate it and by when, which generates a matrix line. Read backward, each planned study raises the question of which claim it supports and which decision it informs, which prunes studies that serve neither. Building the HTA and payer perspective into the TPP early, as Wang and colleagues recommend, is what prevents a program from reaching approval with a strong regulatory dossier but no evidence for the comparator and outcomes an access decision will require ([Wang et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2022.948161)).

## 10.8 Governance, review cadence, and impact measurement

A plan that is written once and shelved fails, because evidence needs, competitors, and decisions change. Governance keeps the plan live.

Common failure modes explain why governance is necessary rather than optional. Evidence plans fail when:

- no single forum owns the plan, so it fragments back into functional plans;
- the plan is written at launch and never revised as decisions and competitors change;
- gaps are catalogued but never prioritized, so resources spread thinly;
- studies are timed to functional cycles rather than to the decisions they must inform; and
- success is measured by studies completed rather than by decisions influenced.

Governance is designed to prevent each of these:

- **Ownership and forum.** A cross-functional evidence-generation governance body (clinical development, medical affairs, HEOR, regulatory, and market access) owns the plan, resolves competing priorities, and holds the authority to commit or stop studies. Without a single owning forum, the plan reverts to a collection of functional plans.
- **Review cadence.** The plan is reviewed on a defined schedule and at trigger events: a trial readout, a new competitor entry, an HTA decision, a label change, a guideline revision, or a new post-approval commitment. Each review re-runs the gap assessment and re-prioritizes, so that the plan reflects the current decision landscape rather than the one that existed when it was written.
- **Impact measurement.** The plan measures whether its evidence changed decisions, not only whether studies were completed. This mirrors the distinction between activity metrics and outcome metrics that recurs across medical affairs, where counting outputs is easier than demonstrating that an output influenced a decision ([MAPS, Challenges to Opportunities](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Challenges-to-Opportunities.pdf)).

Credible impact metrics include whether an evidence deliverable was cited in an HTA appraisal, resolved a payer's stated uncertainty, supported a coverage or pricing decision, changed a guideline recommendation, or satisfied a managed-access data commitment on time. The impact-metric column in the matrix (section 10.5) is what makes this measurement possible: because each line was tied to a decision when it was planned, a later review can ask whether that decision was in fact informed, rather than settling for a count of completed studies.

Each trigger event has a defined review action, so that the plan responds to change rather than drifting:

| Trigger event | Review action |
|---|---|
| A trial reads out | Update the matrix and the dynamic TPP; retire or re-scope affected lines |
| A competitor enters or reports data | Re-run the gap assessment against the new comparator landscape |
| An HTA or payer decision is issued | Record the impact metric; add any evidence the decision left outstanding |
| A label or guideline changes | Re-check which claims still require substantiation |
| A managed-access commitment is agreed | Add the committed study to the matrix with its owner and deadline |

## 10.9 The role of AI in evidence mapping

AI can accelerate the parts of evidence planning that are search- and synthesis-intensive: scanning the literature and trial registries to map the current landscape, clustering evidence to surface candidate gaps, extracting study characteristics into evidence tables, and drafting first-pass syntheses and matrix rows for human review. ISPOR's working-group reports describe where generative AI can support HTA and evidence work and, equally, where it introduces risks of fabrication, bias, and non-reproducibility that require validation before use ([Fleurence et al., 2025, generative AI for HTA](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2024.10.3846); [Fleurence et al., 2025, taxonomy of generative AI in HEOR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2025.04.2167)). Where machine-learning methods are used in the evidence itself rather than only to draft it, a good-practices checklist governs their reporting and appraisal ([Padula et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2022.03.022)).

The division of labor is the point. AI can propose a landscape and flag candidate gaps at a speed no human can match, but prioritization, the judgment of decision impact, and the commitment of studies remain human and cross-functional decisions. A tool that invents a citation or misstates a trial result will corrupt a gap assessment if its output is trusted unverified. The following division makes the boundary concrete:

| AI-assisted task | What it contributes | Required human control |
|---|---|---|
| Literature and registry landscape mapping | Speed and breadth of coverage | Verify every source and result against the primary record |
| Clustering evidence to surface candidate gaps | Candidate gaps a reviewer might miss | Confirm each gap blocks a real decision before acting |
| Drafting evidence tables from source documents | A first-pass structured extraction | Check every extracted value against the source |
| Prioritizing and committing studies | Not delegated | Cross-functional judgment of decision impact and cost |

Adoption of these tools in medical affairs is uneven and governance-dependent, as the MAPS benchmark on digital, advanced analytics, and AI documents ([MAPS, Digital, Advanced Analytics and AI in Medical Affairs report, 2024](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/MAPS-Digital-Advanced-Analytics-Artificial-Intelligence-Report-2024.pdf)).

Every AI-assisted step in the plan is verified against primary sources and recorded in the AI-use log. For each use, the log records:

- the tool and the task it performed;
- the inputs provided and whether any were confidential;
- what a human verified and against which source; and
- who is accountable for the output.

This documentation is not incidental to the plan; it is part of it, and it is the standard the AI governance module (Module 14) defines for the whole portfolio.

## Worked example: an evidence matrix for the capstone product

The capstone product scenario (case J in `assessment/case_library.md`) specifies a therapeutic area, a target product profile, and an evidence base with deliberate gaps and stakeholder needs; it is the intended subject of the plan. The table below shows four illustrative rows of an integrated evidence matrix. It is a template to be populated with the assigned product's specifics, not a finished plan.

| Evidence question | Study concept | Data source | Timing | Owner | Decision point | Impact metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| What is the comparative effectiveness of the product versus the payer's standard-of-care comparator, which was not studied head to head? | Indirect treatment comparison / network meta-analysis of existing trials | Published pivotal and comparator trials identified by systematic review | Before first HTA submission | HEOR | HTA appraisal and formulary review | Indirect comparison accepted by the committee; uncertainty on the relative effect resolved |
| Does the trial benefit persist in routine-care patients excluded from the pivotal trial (for example older or comorbid subgroups)? | Real-world comparative study framed as a target-trial emulation | Claims or electronic-health-record database | 12 to 24 months after launch | Epidemiology / RWE | Guideline update; payer re-review | Evidence cited in guideline or coverage decision; effectiveness in the subgroup quantified |
| Is the product a cost-effective use of resources at the proposed price versus the comparator? | Cost-utility model reported against CHEERS | Trial efficacy, health-state utilities, and local unit costs | Before HTA submission | HEOR / market access | HTA appraisal and price negotiation | Incremental cost-effectiveness result supports the value-based price; managed-entry terms informed |
| Which outcomes matter most to patients, and are they captured by current endpoints? | Patient-preference and patient-reported-outcome study | Primary data collection with patient involvement | During late development, before label discussions | Medical affairs / patient engagement | Endpoint selection; label and value narrative | Patient-relevant outcome added to the evidence base and to the dynamic TPP |

Each row states a question a real stakeholder decision requires, assigns a fit-for-purpose study concept and a named data source, ties the timing to the decision, gives the line a single owner, names the decision it feeds, and defines how the plan will know the evidence mattered. Read together, the rows also show the coordination the matrix is meant to produce: the indirect comparison and the economic model both feed the same HTA appraisal, the patient-outcome study feeds both the label and the dynamic TPP, and the real-world study is timed to a post-launch decision rather than the filing.

To populate the matrix for the assigned product, a learner works from the scenario's stated gaps and stakeholder needs and, for each prioritized question, decides:

- which specific decision the answer will inform, and by when;
- which study concept is both fit for purpose and feasible with an available data source;
- which single function should own the line; and
- what observation would show, after the fact, that the evidence changed the decision.

The rows also illustrate the sequencing discipline from section 10.4. Two of the four questions (the indirect comparison and the economic model) must be answered before the first HTA submission, while the durability question can only be answered after launch and is therefore tied to a later re-review. A plan that placed the durability study before launch would be infeasible; a plan that omitted it would leave a foreseeable challenge unanswered at re-review. Populating the matrix for the assigned product, defending the prioritization behind it, and defending the timing of each line is the substance of the deliverable.

## Applied activity (produces capstone work product 5)

Using the assigned capstone product scenario (case J), produce a full integrated evidence generation plan outline. It has three parts:

1. **An evidence-gap assessment.** The stakeholder decisions the product must satisfy, the evidence each requires, and the prioritized gaps, with the rationale for the ranking (sections 10.3 and 10.4).
2. **An integrated evidence matrix.** The seven columns above, populated with the product's prioritized questions and a defensible ranking, with at least one line each for a regulatory, an HTA or payer, and a clinician or patient decision.
3. **A governance and impact section.** The review cadence, the owning forum, and the impact metrics by which the plan will judge whether its evidence changed decisions (section 10.8).

Reference the dynamic TPP as the link between the matrix and the product's value proposition (section 10.7), and note where the plan consumes the managed-entry commitments from the Module 9 submission (work product 6). This is capstone work product 5 and a core component of the portfolio.

A strong plan is distinguished from a weak one less by length than by whether it demonstrates:

- every prioritized gap is tied to a named decision, not to a general wish for more data;
- every matrix line has one accountable owner and a timing anchored to its decision;
- the study concept for each line is feasible given the named data source;
- the prioritization is defended, including what was deferred or dropped and why;
- each claim in the dynamic TPP maps to a matrix line, and each line maps to a claim; and
- the impact metrics would let a later review judge whether the evidence changed the decision.

A plan that satisfies these reads as a strategy; one that lists studies without decisions, owners, or metrics reads as an inventory.

## AI-use focus

Permitted: use an AI assistant to map the evidence landscape from the literature and trial registries, to cluster and surface candidate evidence gaps, and to draft first-pass evidence tables and matrix rows for human review.

Required controls: verify every extracted fact, citation, and study characteristic against its primary source, because generative tools fabricate references and misstate results ([Fleurence et al., 2025, generative AI for HTA](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2024.10.3846)); keep prioritization, decision-impact judgment, and study commitment as human, cross-functional decisions; do not enter confidential product or pricing information into an unvalidated tool; and record each AI use in the plan's audit log with the source verified against and the accountable person. This applies the program AI-use policy (`governance/ai_use_policy_and_playbook.md`) and is the standard defined in Module 14.

4. List the columns of an integrated evidence matrix and state what the "impact metric" column is for. (Answer: evidence question, study concept, data source, timing, owner, decision point, and impact metric; the impact metric states how the plan will know the evidence changed a decision, for example being cited in an HTA appraisal or resolving a stated uncertainty.)
5. How does a dynamic target product profile relate to the evidence matrix, and what does a claim with no matching matrix line indicate? (Answer: the TPP states the intended claims and value target and carries HTA and payer requirements into development, while the matrix states the studies that substantiate them; they are two views of one plan, updated together. A claim with no matching line is an aspiration with no evidence route. See Wang et al.)
6. Why does the plan measure decision impact rather than only study completion, and what governance keeps the plan current? (Answer: because completing studies does not demonstrate that evidence influenced decisions, mirroring the activity-versus-outcome metric distinction; a cross-functional governance body reviews the plan on a set cadence and at trigger events such as readouts, competitor entry, and HTA decisions.)
7. Where can AI accelerate evidence planning, and which steps must remain human? (Answer: AI can map the landscape, surface candidate gaps, and draft evidence tables and syntheses; prioritization, decision-impact judgment, study commitment, and verification of every AI output against primary sources remain human and cross-functional.)
8. Why is the plan described as self-checking when the target product profile and the matrix are read together? (Answer: reading the TPP forward turns each intended claim into a required matrix line, and reading the matrix backward prunes any study that supports no claim and informs no decision; a claim with no line, or a line with no claim, is flagged for reconciliation.)

## Connection to the capstone

The integrated evidence generation plan is capstone work product 5 and the spine of the portfolio. It consumes the real-world evidence feasibility work from Module 7, the economic model from Module 8, and the HTA and payer submission from Module 9 (work product 6), and it schedules and owns the evidence those decisions leave outstanding, including any managed-entry data commitments. Its evidence questions and owners connect forward to the field-medical and communication plans (Modules 11 and 12), and its AI-assisted steps are logged under the plan built in Module 14. It is the deliverable in which the separate methods of Domain C are shown to work as one strategy, which is why it is weighted heavily in the capstone rubric.

Completing this plan is the clearest single demonstration of Domain C and Domain G together: the command of evidence methods from Modules 7 to 9, and the strategic judgment to direct those methods at the specific decisions that determine whether a product reaches patients. A candidate who can build and defend an integrated evidence generation plan can do the work this program exists to certify.
