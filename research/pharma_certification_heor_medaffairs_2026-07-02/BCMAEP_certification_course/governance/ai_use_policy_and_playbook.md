# BCMAEP AI-Use Policy and Playbook

This document defines how artificial intelligence (AI) is used within the BCMAEP curriculum and how learners are expected to use AI in the work products they produce. It is a program-wide policy, referenced by every module and enforced in the capstone. Module 14 teaches the concepts; this document is the operative standard.

The aim is to prepare learners to use AI the way a compliant, well-run medical and evidence organization expects: as an assistant that increases speed and coverage, under controls that preserve accuracy, privacy, disclosure, and human accountability. The policy is written to align with current regulatory and professional guidance, cited below.

---

## 1. Principles

Six principles govern all AI use in the program and in learner work products.

1. **Human accountability.** A named person is accountable for every AI-assisted output. AI tools are not authors and cannot bear responsibility; publication-ethics standards are explicit that chatbots cannot be listed as authors because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the work ([ICMJE, Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html)).
2. **Source verification.** Every factual claim and every citation produced with AI assistance is verified against a primary source before use. Generative models can produce fluent but fabricated references and facts; verification is the control that manages this risk.
3. **Privacy and confidentiality.** Patient-level data, personally identifiable information, and confidential or proprietary company information are not entered into AI tools unless the tool is validated, approved, and contractually governed for that purpose. Default to exclusion when in doubt.
4. **Disclosure.** AI assistance is disclosed where the output is shared, consistent with journal and society expectations. GPP 2022 addresses the compliant incorporation of new and emerging publication tools ([DeTora et al., 2022, GPP 2022](https://doi.org/10.7326/M22-1460)), and the ICMJE recommendations require disclosure of AI-assisted technologies at submission ([ICMJE](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html)).
5. **Fitness, fairness, and bias.** An AI tool is evaluated for fitness for the specific task and for bias before it is relied upon; outputs affecting people or evidence are checked for systematic error.
6. **Reproducibility and documentation.** AI use is documented so that a colleague or reviewer can understand what was done and, where relevant, reproduce it. Transparency checklists exist for machine-learning work in HEOR and should be applied ([Padula et al., 2022, PALISADE checklist, ISPOR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2022.03.022)).

## 2. The guidance landscape learners must know

Learners are expected to situate their AI use within current guidance. None of these documents is a course requirement to memorize; the requirement is to know they exist, what they say at a principle level, and where to find them.

- **FDA.** In January 2025, the FDA issued draft guidance, "Considerations for the Use of Artificial Intelligence To Support Regulatory Decision-Making for Drug and Biological Products," its first guidance on AI in drug and biologic development; the availability notice was published in the Federal Register on January 7, 2025 ([Federal Register, FDA AI draft guidance notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/07/2024-31542/considerations-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-to-support-regulatory-decision-making-for-drug)). It proposes a risk-based credibility assessment for an AI model tied to a defined context of use, spanning nonclinical, clinical, postmarketing, and manufacturing phases. This document remains a draft (its comment period closed in April 2025) and is labeled non-binding and not for implementation. In January 2026, the FDA (CDER and CBER) and the EMA jointly published a set of ten "Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice in Drug Development," applying a human-centric, risk-based approach across the development lifecycle ([FDA, Guiding Principles of Good AI Practice in Drug Development](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/artificial-intelligence-drug-development/guiding-principles-good-ai-practice-drug-development)).
- **EMA.** The European Medicines Agency's "Reflection paper on the use of Artificial Intelligence in the medicinal product lifecycle" (EMA/CHMP/CVMP/83833/2023), first published on September 30, 2024, is adopted as final and states principles for applying AI and machine learning at any step of the medicine lifecycle ([EMA, use of AI in the medicinal product lifecycle](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/use-artificial-intelligence-ai-medicinal-product-lifecycle)). The EMA and the Heads of Medicines Agencies maintain a multiannual AI workplan and publish annual AI monitoring reports.
- **ISPOR (HEOR).** The PALISADE checklist is a good-practices report for machine-learning methods in HEOR and provides a transparency checklist across five application areas ([Padula et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2022.03.022)). ISPOR Working Group reports also address generative AI: one on opportunities, challenges, and policy considerations for HTA, emphasizing human oversight ([Fleurence et al., 2025, generative AI for HTA](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2024.10.3846)); one defining a taxonomy of generative AI concepts for HEOR such as prompt engineering, retrieval-augmented generation, fine-tuning, and agents, with current limitations ([Fleurence et al., 2025, taxonomy of generative AI in HEOR](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2025.04.2167)); and a 2025 reporting guideline, ELEVATE-GenAI, for the use of large language models in HEOR ([Fleurence et al., 2025, ELEVATE-GenAI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2025.06.018)).
- **Publications.** GPP 2022 covers ethics, transparency, and the compliant incorporation of new and emerging publication tools ([DeTora et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.7326/M22-1460)); the ICMJE recommendations place responsibility for AI-assisted content, including plagiarism and accuracy review, on the human authors ([ICMJE](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html)).
- **Medical affairs practice.** MAPS has published a position white paper on generative AI in medical affairs and a 2024 benchmark report on digital, advanced analytics, and AI in medical affairs surveying 32 companies ([MAPS, Digital, Advanced Analytics and AI in Medical Affairs benchmark report, 2024](https://medicalaffairs.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/MAPS-Digital-Advanced-Analytics-Artificial-Intelligence-Report-2024.pdf)).

## 3. Permitted, conditional, and prohibited uses

The table classifies common tasks. "Conditional" means permitted only with the stated control. This table is applied in every module's applied activity.

| Task | Classification | Required control |
|---|---|---|
| Summarizing published literature to orient a search | Permitted | Verify all facts and citations against primary sources before use |
| Drafting an outline for a work product | Permitted | Human authorship and editing; no unverified claims carried forward |
| Extracting study characteristics into an evidence table | Conditional | Verify every extracted value against the source article |
| Drafting code lists or phenotype definitions for RWE | Conditional | Validate every definition against source documentation |
| Drafting a standard medical-information response | Conditional | Verify every reference and fact; disclose AI assistance; medical review before release |
| Structuring or documenting an economic model | Conditional | Independently verify model logic and inputs; apply the PALISADE transparency checklist where machine learning is used |
| Drafting a manuscript or abstract with AI assistance | Conditional | Disclose per journal and GPP 2022; human authors accountable; AI not an author; verify all references |
| Entering patient-level or confidential company data into a general AI tool | Prohibited | Use only a validated, approved, contractually governed environment |
| Presenting AI output as fact without verification | Prohibited | Not permitted under any circumstance |
| Automated safety adjudication without human sign-off | Prohibited | Pharmacovigilance decisions require validated systems and human accountability |
| Fabricating or guessing a citation, DOI, or data point | Prohibited | Not permitted; mark a gap instead |

## 4. The AI-use audit log

Every learner maintains an AI-use log across the portfolio (capstone work product 8). Each material AI use is one row.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Date | When the AI was used |
| Work product | Which deliverable the use supported |
| Tool | The specific tool or model and version |
| Task | What the AI was asked to do |
| Data handling | What data were entered; confirmation that no confidential or patient data entered a non-approved tool |
| Verification | What was checked, against which primary source, and the result |
| Changes made | How the AI output was edited or corrected by the human |
| Accountable person | The named person responsible for the final output |
| Disclosure | Where and how the AI use was disclosed, if the output was shared |

A portfolio that used AI without a log is returned. A log that shows unverified claims carried into a deliverable fails the compliance and evidence dimensions of the rubric.

## 5. Evaluating an AI tool or workflow

Before relying on an AI tool for a task, the learner answers, and documents:

1. **Fitness:** is this tool designed and validated for this task, or is it being used outside its intended purpose?
2. **Data governance:** where do inputs and outputs go, who can see them, and are confidentiality and privacy preserved?
3. **Accuracy and hallucination risk:** how are factual claims and citations verified, and what is the observed error rate on a check set?
4. **Bias:** could the tool systematically err for some groups or inputs, and how is that checked?
5. **Reproducibility:** can the process be described and, where needed, repeated; are prompts and settings recorded?
6. **Accountability:** who signs off, and what is the escalation path if the tool fails?

This checklist is the deliverable for the Module 14 tool-evaluation activity and is a required part of the capstone AI-use plan.

## 6. Governance and roles in practice

Learners should understand how a compliant organization governs AI, because they will work within such a system:

- An accountable function (often medical, regulatory, or a dedicated committee) approves tools and use cases.
- Validated environments exist for confidential or regulated data; general tools are restricted to non-confidential tasks.
- Human review is required before external release of AI-assisted content, and disclosure follows the applicable standard.
- Use is logged and auditable, and the policy is reviewed as guidance evolves. Because FDA, EMA, ISPOR, and publication bodies have all issued or updated AI guidance recently, the field is treated as fast-moving, and the program requires at least three hours of AI-governance continuing education per recertification cycle (handbook, section 10).

## 7. Why this is a differentiator

BCMAEP makes AI governance an operative policy that is applied in every module and audited in the capstone, grounded in the specific, current regulatory and professional guidance above. The intended outcome is a graduate who can use AI productively and defend how they used it to a compliance officer, an editor, and an HTA reviewer.
